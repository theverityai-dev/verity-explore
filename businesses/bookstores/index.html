How it runs today

Most bookstores are run by someone who loves books and keeps score in a notebook.

The shop is easy to see. The new releases table, the children’s corner, the queue of parents with school lists in June. What is hard to see is everything behind it: which of the five thousand titles has not sold in a year, which publisher’s stock must go back before the return window closes, which special order is still waiting, and what each publisher is owed.

That information exists in pieces. The billing screen knows the sale. Publisher invoices and return notes are in files. School lists are photographed on WhatsApp. Special orders are on slips of paper. The owner holds the rest in memory.

The market is large and changing. A 2026 industry report puts book publishing at about ₹1.08 lakh crore in 2024-25, with more than 26,000 publishers, and one market research estimate puts nearly 44 percent of book sales through online channels. A bookshop that knows its readers and its stock holds the edge that no website has.

What most bookstores run on

  • Billing screen at the counter
  • Publisher and distributor invoices and return notes
  • Notebook or Excel for special orders and school lists
  • WhatsApp for readers, schools and sales reps
  • The owner’s memory of what sells

Each one does its own job well enough. None of them talk to each other, and nobody has time to join them.

Where a bookstore actually loses money.

These are the situations bookshop owners and managers describe again and again. For each one: what it looks like on an ordinary day, what it costs, and what Verity does about it.

  1. 01Titles

    A customer asks for a title and nobody knows if you have it

    What it looks like

    A reader asks for a specific novel. Staff walk the shelves, check the till, ask the owner. The book is in the stock room under a different shelf label, or sold last week, or never ordered. The reader leaves with the same question.

    What it costs you

    Example arithmetic: ten such lost sales a week at an average of ₹450 is ₹4,500 a week, about ₹2.3 lakh a year, and some readers do not come back.

    How Verity fixes it

    Every title is a record with its ISBN, author, publisher, price, shelf and copies on hand. Staff search once and see whether it is in, where, and if not, which supplier can deliver and when.

    See the screen
  2. 02Titles

    Dead titles that tie up cash for years

    What it looks like

    A hardback bought in bulk after a review, a series nobody wanted, last year’s exam guides. They sit on the shelf for a year and then go to a corner. Nobody has looked at the list of what has not sold.

    What it costs you

    A bookstore’s profit is in the turn of its stock. Each slow title holds shelf space and cash that a faster title could use.

    How Verity fixes it

    Verity lists titles by days since the last sale and by value held. Slow movers show with a suggested action, such as a face-out, a bundle, a return to the publisher where the terms allow it or a markdown, and an owner for each.

    See the screen
  3. 03Publishers

    Returns to publishers that go back too late

    What it looks like

    A publisher takes unsold copies back within a set time. The window closes while you are busy with school season. The unsold pile stays, and the publisher’s statement still lists it against you.

    What it costs you

    One publishing executive, quoted in a trade article, described the usual terms as sale-or-return, with retailers holding payment against expected returns. Missing a window turns a return into a loss.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each publisher account records its terms: discount, credit days and return window. Verity lists titles approaching the end of their return window with copies unsold and prepares the return list with the publisher’s reference, so nothing goes late.

    See the screen
  4. 04Publishers

    Publisher statements that do not match what you received

    What it looks like

    The distributor’s statement lists forty copies of a title. Thirty-six arrived. A credit note for last season’s returns is missing. The statement is paid because it is easier than checking it.

    What it costs you

    You pay for books that never arrived, and your accountant rebuilds receipts, returns and credits at year end.

    How Verity fixes it

    Deliveries are received against the order by title and copy count. Verity compares order, receipt, return and statement for each publisher and shows the balance owed, with every difference marked before you pay.

    See the screen
  5. 05School season

    School lists in April and no way to plan for them

    What it looks like

    The school sends its book list a few weeks before term. Parents arrive in a rush in the same week. You order from the publisher, who is out of stock on two titles. A parent goes to the shop across the road and buys the whole set there.

    What it costs you

    Educational titles are a large part of the market, about 35 percent in one estimate, and the school season is when a bookshop earns a big part of its year. A set sold elsewhere is a set lost.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each school’s list is a record with titles, classes and expected sets. Verity compares the list to stock and open orders, shows what is short, and tracks the supplier’s delivery date so you can tell the school and parents before they ask.

    See the screen
  6. 06School season

    Pre-booked sets and parents who ask the same question ten times

    What it looks like

    Parents pre-book sets on a phone call. The booking sits in a notebook. When the set is ready nobody calls, and the parent turns up on the last day to find it unclear whether it was kept aside.

    What it costs you

    Pre-booked sets are the surest sales you have. A forgotten call is a sale handed to a competitor.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each set booking is a record with parent, class, titles, advance paid and status. Verity shows what is packed, what is waiting on a title and what has not been collected, and gives the counter a task to call.

    See the screen
  7. 07Orders

    Special orders that are placed and then forgotten

    What it looks like

    A reader asks for a title you do not stock. You note it on a slip, order it with the next supplier delivery, and the book arrives two weeks later. Nobody calls the reader, and the slip is lost.

    What it costs you

    A special order is the strongest signal a customer can give. Forgetting it costs the sale and the relationship.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each special order is a record with the reader, the title, the supplier, the date ordered and the expected date. When the book arrives, the counter gets a task to call, and if the date slips, the reader is told.

    See the screen
  8. 08Orders

    Library, college and corporate orders lost in the inbox

    What it looks like

    A college librarian sends a list of two hundred titles. A company wants forty books as gifts. The replies are slow, the quote is built by hand and the details are in three chats and an email.

    What it costs you

    Institutional orders are the largest single sales a bookshop sees, and they are lost to slow replies far more often than to price.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each enquiry is a record with an owner, the list, the quote, the discount and the next step. Verity lists the ones with no reply and tells the owner of that enquiry. Staff still send the quote, and the outcome is recorded.

    See the screen
  9. 09Readers

    Regulars the shop knows by face and not by record

    What it looks like

    A reader who bought every new crime novel for three years has not been in since March. A new book by the same author arrives and no one thinks of her. The staff member who knew her taste has left.

    What it costs you

    Repeat readers are the heart of an independent bookshop. Without a record of what each one reads, a bookseller’s recommendation cannot outlast the person.

    How Verity fixes it

    Purchases link to a reader’s record with the authors and genres they buy. When a new title matches, or a regular goes quiet beyond their usual gap, a task goes to the person who knows them.

  10. 10Readers

    A bookseller’s knowledge walks out with the bookseller

    What it looks like

    The staff member who knew the shop’s poetry shelf and the old customers moves to another shop. The recommendations, the sorted shelves and the contact numbers go with them.

    What it costs you

    Training time and a quiet fall in sales for a few months in the section they looked after.

    How Verity fixes it

    Reader preferences, shelf owners and notes sit as records in the shop’s system, not in one person’s phone. A handover list is ready the day someone resigns, and sections can be reassigned in one step.

    See the screen
  11. 11Money

    Online discounts and a margin that nobody has calculated

    What it looks like

    A customer shows you a lower price on a website and asks you to match it. Each publisher gives you a different discount, and one gives you almost nothing. You match the price without knowing what you earn on the title.

    What it costs you

    Booksellers have said that the discount they receive does not add up to much given book prices, and online discounts are a concern. Matching a price on a thin-margin title can mean selling at a loss.

    How Verity fixes it

    Every title carries its purchase discount from its publisher. Verity shows margin per title, per publisher and per category, flags a match below your floor, and lets you set an approval limit for price matches.

    See the screen
  12. 12Money

    Stationery, gifts and cafe sales mixed into one number

    What it looks like

    Printed books are generally exempt from GST, while stationery, toys and gifts carry GST at their own rates. The bill has all of them, and the accountant has to separate the lines by hand.

    What it costs you

    Hours of correction every month, and a risk of a wrong rate on a bill.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each item carries its tax category, so a mixed bill is split by line as it is made. The accountant sees books and other goods separately at month end, with no re-keying.

    See the screen
  13. 13Money

    Day-end takings do not tie to the bills

    What it looks like

    At closing, cash, card and UPI are counted against the billing report and are off by a few hundred rupees. Nobody can say if a bill was missed, a refund was given or the float was wrong.

    What it costs you

    Small daily gaps compound, and the owner stops trusting the numbers on the screen.

    How Verity fixes it

    The day’s sales by payment mode are compared with what was collected. A mismatch is flagged with the shift and amount, and the person closing records the reason.

    See the screen
  14. 14Owner

    Book fairs, school stalls and a second shop

    What it looks like

    You take a stall at a school fair or a book festival with a van of stock. Half comes back unlabelled. A second branch keeps its own notebook, and a reader who buys at both is two different people in your records.

    What it costs you

    Stock that goes out and does not return in count, and no fair comparison between shops or events.

    How Verity fixes it

    Stalls and branches are locations in one system. Stock leaves and returns against a recorded transfer, the stall’s sales are counted against what went out, and a reader is one person across all of them.

Rules and paperwork

The rules a bookstore has to keep up with.

Verity does not give legal or tax advice and does not file anything for you. It keeps the records these rules depend on in order, so your accountant is not rebuilding them from a bill book.

  • GST on books and other goods

    What is expected

    Printed books are generally exempt from GST, while stationery, toys and gift items carry GST at their own rates, and printing services and paper used by publishers are taxed. E-books are generally taxed differently. Check each item’s category.

    How Verity helps

    Each item carries its tax category, so a mixed bill is split by line and the accountant sees books and other goods separately.

    PrintWeek India, 2025

  • Selling at or below the printed price

    What is expected

    The Legal Metrology rules generally bar a dealer from selling a packed commodity above its printed retail price, with narrow exceptions for a valid tax revision.

    How Verity helps

    Each item records its printed price and the price charged, and a bill above it is flagged before it closes.

    IndiaLaw, 2025

  • Reader data and consent

    What is expected

    Phone numbers, addresses and reading history are personal data. India’s data protection rules were notified on 13 November 2025, and most duties start in phases, with the main ones about 18 months later.

    How Verity helps

    Reader data sits in one permissioned place, with who viewed or changed it recorded, instead of in personal phones and notebooks.

    AZB Partners, 2025

  • Shops and establishments registration and staff records

    What is expected

    Most states require a shops and establishments registration and records of working hours, weekly offs and leave. Details differ by state, so check your own.

    How Verity helps

    The registration is stored with its renewal date and owner, and shifts, attendance and leave are recorded against each person.

Rules change and differ by state. Confirm current requirements with your accountant or the relevant authority.

Questions

Questions bookstore owners ask.

I have thousands of titles. Will I have to key them all in?

No. We bring in your list from the sheet or billing report you have, and tidy it in stages. Fast-moving and current titles come first, and slow ones are added as they sell or are counted.

Does Verity replace my billing system?

No. Your billing screen keeps taking bills and payments. We agree during setup how those bills reach Verity, which then does the stock, publisher, order and reader work around them.

Can it track returns to publishers?
