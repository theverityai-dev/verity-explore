How it runs today

Most interior design firms win projects on taste and lose margin on coordination.

The design is the visible part: the mood board, the 3D views, the material samples, the final room. What is hard to see is the paperwork around it: which version the client signed off, which BOQ line has gone over, which vendor is late and what that does to the carpenter's slot, and whether the second payment is due.

That information lives in many places. Approvals are in chat threads and in a signed PDF. The BOQ is a spreadsheet that gets copied for each revision. Vendor quotes are in email. Site updates are photos in a WhatsApp group. Payment stages are in the quotation.

A 2026 client-side guide on turnkey interiors tells homeowners never to sign without a BOQ that shows brand, model, unit price and quantity for every line, and to tie each payment to a physical milestone. Clients are being coached to ask for exactly the detail that many studios keep in their heads.

What most interior design firms run on

  • Design software and shared folders
  • Excel for BOQ and budget
  • WhatsApp groups with clients, vendors and site teams
  • Quotation and agreement PDFs
  • Accounting software kept by the accountant

Each one is useful. None of them tells you that the client approved the wardrobe finish on the 12th, the laminate was ordered on the 14th, and the BOQ still shows the old rate.

16 problems, one by one

Where an interior design practice actually loses money and time.

These are the situations studio owners, designers and project managers describe again and again. For each one: what it looks like on an ordinary week, what it costs, and what Verity does about it.

  1. 01Approvals

    Clients approve by chat and later say they never did

    What it looks like

    The client sent a thumbs-up on the kitchen layout in a group chat. Three weeks later, with the carcass made, they say they wanted the sink on the other wall. The message is somewhere in 600 others.

    What it costs you

    Rework on joinery that has already been made is paid for by the studio unless the approval is clear. Example arithmetic: a rebuilt modular kitchen carcass costing ₹1.2 lakh, half of it wasted, is ₹60,000 against a design fee that was perhaps ₹1.5 lakh.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each design item, such as a layout, material or finish, is a record with versions, the client's approval, the date and the person who gave it. Work orders to vendors can start only from an approved item, and the approved version is the one the vendor sees.

    See the screen
  2. 02Approvals

    Revision rounds go past what was agreed

    What it looks like

    The agreement covers three design rounds. The client is in round six for the living room. No one has said so.

    What it costs you

    A published guide for homeowners suggests that more than two to three revision cycles be treated as excluded and priced separately. Rounds that are not counted are rounds that are not charged.

    How Verity fixes it

    Every round of changes is logged against the room with the date and hours spent. When the agreed number is reached, the next round creates a task for the lead designer to quote an extra, and the client's approval is recorded before design work resumes.

    See the screen
  3. 03Approvals

    Selections are late and hold up the whole schedule

    What it looks like

    Tiles, sanitaryware and lighting must be chosen before the civil work ends. The client takes three weeks to decide. The carpenter's slot is booked for the next Monday.

    What it costs you

    A late selection delays everything after it, and the delay is usually blamed on the studio, not the client.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each selection has a needed-by date tied to a site stage. Open ones are flagged before the stage starts, and the task goes to the designer who deals with the client, with the impact on the schedule shown.

    See the screen
  4. 04Budget

    The BOQ in the quote and the BOQ in reality drift apart

    What it looks like

    The quotation was ₹38 lakh. Plywood prices rose, the client upgraded the wardrobe finish and the lights were changed. Nobody has updated the BOQ. At handover the studio's own cost is ₹3 lakh more than the budget.

    What it costs you

    Example arithmetic: a 6 percent drift on a ₹38 lakh job is ₹2.3 lakh. Studios usually find it at handover, when it can no longer be passed on.

    How Verity fixes it

    The BOQ is a record by line with quantity, brand, unit rate and budget. Each purchase and vendor bill is recorded against its line, and Verity shows budget, committed and actual per line and per room, flagging lines going over.

    See the screen
  5. 05Budget

    Variations and extras are agreed in passing

    What it looks like

    The client asks for a niche in the passage and a changed ceiling in the study during a site visit. Both are done the next week. The variation is not priced.

    What it costs you

    Extras that are done and not billed cost the studio materials, labour and goodwill when it is finally raised.

    How Verity fixes it

    A variation is a record with the request, the estimate and the client's approval. Work is marked uninstructed until approved, and approved variations add to the BOQ and to the next payment stage.

    See the screen
  6. 06Budget

    Material rates change after the quote

    What it looks like

    The quote was priced in July. By October laminates, hardware and plywood cost more. The studio had promised a fixed price. The rate difference is absorbed.

    What it costs you

    A fixed price in a rising market shrinks the margin purchase by purchase. A client guide in 2026 suggests capping rate escalation at 5 percent or fixing the price in writing.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each purchase records the rate against the BOQ rate. Verity flags lines where the rate paid is above the one quoted, with the quantity still to buy, so you can ask the client for a revision or change the specification while it is still possible.

    See the screen
  7. 07Vendors

    Vendors are late and the site waits

    What it looks like

    The sofa is delayed by two weeks, the stone for the counter has not been cut, and the electrician has gone to another site. The designer rearranges the plan by phone for the third time.

    What it costs you

    Every day of overrun is a day of rent for the client and a day of site cost for the studio, plus an unhappy customer.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each order has a vendor, an expected date and a promised date, with the site stage that depends on it. Verity lists orders at risk with the stage they will hold up, and records the vendor's reply and new date.

    See the screen
  8. 08Vendors

    Vendor quotes, orders and payments are scattered

    What it looks like

    The carpenter's quote is on email, the order on WhatsApp, the advance by UPI and the bill on paper. At month end nobody can say how much has been paid for which project.

    What it costs you

    Duplicated payments, advances forgotten and disputes with vendors.

    How Verity fixes it

    A vendor order is a record with the project, BOQ line, quote, advance paid, delivery and bill. Payments are made against orders, and the studio sees what is owed to each vendor and for which project.

    See the screen
  9. 09Site

    Site stages overlap and trades collide

    What it looks like

    The painters arrive while the false ceiling team is still working. The polishing team finds the floor not protected. The site supervisor sorts it by shouting.

    What it costs you

    Rework and damage to finished surfaces, and a schedule that slips by days each time.

    How Verity fixes it

    The site has a stage plan with the trades and dates, shown on one schedule. Overlaps are flagged and the site supervisor sees what is next for each trade, with the dependency on materials and approvals.

    See the screen
  10. 10Site

    Site updates are photos nobody can search

    What it looks like

    The client asks what the site looked like before the false ceiling. The designer scrolls through a WhatsApp group of 1,800 photos.

    What it costs you

    Hours lost searching, and a weaker position in any later dispute about what was done and when.

    How Verity fixes it

    Site visits are records with date, room, photos and notes. Issues raised become tasks for a trade with a due date, and a client update can be put together from the record.

    See the screen
  11. 11Money

    Payments are asked for by date, not by milestone

    What it looks like

    The second instalment is due when the carpentry is installed. The studio realises it two weeks after the carpentry is finished. The client has already moved to the next request.

    What it costs you

    Example arithmetic: a 30 percent stage on a ₹38 lakh job is ₹11.4 lakh. Two weeks late on a studio's own borrowed cash is a real cost, and a stage not asked for in time is often paid in parts.

    How Verity fixes it

    The payment schedule is a set of stages tied to site milestones. When a milestone is marked done, a payment request task goes to accounts with the amount and the evidence, and the stage stays red on the owner's screen until paid.

    See the screen
  12. 12Money

    Advances are large and the work is thin

    What it looks like

    A studio takes 50 percent upfront for a fit-out because the client agrees. Work starts slowly. The client asks for a refund after four weeks and the studio has already paid vendor advances.

    What it costs you

    A published guide notes that capping the upfront payment at 20 to 25 percent and tying later payments to physical milestones is the usual advice to clients. Studios that collect more take on more risk. In 2026 a lawyer's article described stalled interior jobs with large advances ending in consumer complaints.

    How Verity fixes it

    The schedule shows advance, milestones and final retention. Verity compares money collected with work completed and vendor money committed, and flags projects where the studio holds less than it has committed.

    See the screen
  13. 13Handover

    Snags and the final payment fight each other

    What it looks like

    At handover the client lists forty snags: a scratched panel, a loose handle, a mismatched paint patch. The final payment of 10 percent is held until all are done. Weeks pass.

    What it costs you

    Final payments held for months, and a client who tells friends the studio does not finish. Retention is normal; open-ended retention is not.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each snag is a record with photo, room, trade, owner and due date. The client's sign-off on each is recorded, and the final payment task is tied to the list so both sides can see what is left.

    See the screen
  14. 14Handover

    Complaints after handover have no history

    What it looks like

    Six months later a drawer sags and the paint peels near a window. The project manager has left. The client calls the designer and nobody knows who made what.

    What it costs you

    A complaint that is handled badly can become a legal one. Stalled or deficient interior work can be taken to a consumer commission as deficiency in service.

    How Verity fixes it

    Handover closes the project but keeps its full record: approvals, BOQ, vendors, site photos and snags. A defect call is logged on the project, goes to the vendor who made that item, and has a due date.

    See the screen
  15. 15New work

    Enquiries and quotes go cold

    What it looks like

    A couple visits the studio, likes the portfolio and asks for a quote. The quote takes ten days. They sign with another studio that replied in two.

    What it costs you

    A project is won on responsiveness as much as on taste. Each quote is hours of designer time that is wasted when it is not followed up.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each enquiry is a record with source, site, budget, scope and next step. Verity lists quotes sent and not followed up, and records the outcome and the reason for loss.

    See the screen
  16. 16Owner

    The owner cannot see the whole studio

    What it looks like

    The principal moves between a site, a vendor showroom and a client meeting. Twelve projects run in parallel and each designer manages four.

    What it costs you

    A designer overloaded on four projects makes a mistake on one. The owner finds out at the complaint.

    How Verity fixes it

    One owner view shows each project's stage, approvals pending, BOQ drift, vendor delays, payment stages due and each designer's load, with the person responsible and the next step.

Rules and paperwork

The rules an interior design practice has to keep up with.

Verity does not give legal or tax advice and does not file anything for you. It keeps the records these rules depend on in order, so you are not hunting for them when someone asks.

  • GST on design and execution

    What is expected

    Interior design consultancy and interior work with materials and labour are generally taxed at 18 percent, and composite interior work is generally treated as a works contract. Whether you must register depends on your turnover and state, and the classification of each job is for your accountant.

    How Verity helps

    Each project records the treatment your accountant sets, and design fees and execution invoices are kept as separate lines so the client sees what each covers.

    Busy, GST on interior work

  • Consumer protection

    What is expected

    A consumer can complain about deficient or delayed service under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019. A lawyer's 2026 article describes interior jobs that stalled after large advances being taken to the District Commission, which generally hears claims up to Rs 50 lakh.

    How Verity helps

    The approvals, BOQ, vendor orders, site photos and payments for each project are kept with dates, so the studio can show what was agreed and delivered.

    Sudhir Rao, Advocate

  • Written scope, BOQ and payment terms

    What is expected

    Disputes in interiors generally come down to what was agreed. A 2026 guide for clients advises a BOQ with brand, model, unit price and quantity per line, revision cycles defined in writing, and payments tied to physical milestones. Your agreement is a contract, so have your lawyer review its terms.

    How Verity helps

    The BOQ, revision rules and payment stages from the agreement are recorded on the project, and changes are recorded as variations with the client's approval.

    Studio Matrx, Turnkey interiors in India

  • Local building and society permissions

    What is expected

    Apartment societies, local authorities and fire rules can require permission before certain works such as structural changes, working hours or waste removal. Rules differ by society and city.

    How Verity helps

    Each project has a checklist of permissions with owners and dates, and work tasks wait for them where you decide.

Rules change and differ by state. Confirm current requirements with your accountant and your lawyer.

Questions

Questions interior design firms ask.

Does Verity replace my design and rendering software?

No. You keep designing where you design. Verity records the approvals, versions, BOQ, vendor orders, site work and payments around the design.

We are a four-person studio. Is this too much?

The same approvals, BOQ drift and payment stages need tracking at any size. We set up only what you need in the same four weeks and leave out the rest.

Can clients approve designs inside Verity?

Approval is recorded by your team against the item, with the client's sign-off attached, such as a signed PDF or a message. Clients do not log in unless you decide so during setup.

Can it keep my BOQ and show what I actually spent?

Yes. Each line has a budget and the purchases and bills recorded against it, so committed and actual cost show per line and per room.

Does it place orders with my vendors?

No. Your team places orders. Verity records each one with the promised date, the BOQ line and the stage it holds, and flags those at risk.

How do you handle projects already half done?

We bring each live project in at its current stage with the latest approvals, the BOQ as it stands, open vendor orders and the payment stages already paid. Finished projects can come later as archives.

Can it help with the final payment and snags?

Yes. Each snag has a trade, a due date and the client's sign-off, and the final payment task is tied to the list. The client still decides when to pay.

I manage projects for architects and builders too. Is that different?

The records are the same with different parties. Each project has its own approval steps and payment terms.

Who can see my data?

Only the people you allow. Designers see their projects, vendors see their own orders, and the principal and accountant see money. Every change is recorded with who made it and when.

What does it cost?

It depends on the number of people, projects and what you want set up. Message us and we will give you a clear number after a short conversation about how your studio runs.
