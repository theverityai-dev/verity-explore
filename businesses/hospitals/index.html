How it runs today

Most hospitals run on several systems and a lot of phone calls.

The front desk registers the patient. The doctor sees them in OPD and may admit. The nurse tracks the ward on a board. The billing desk builds a bill from slips. The insurance desk chases the insurer. The pharmacy and stores count in their own register. The quality coordinator assembles files before an audit. Each team does its part. The patient moves through all of them.

What is hard to see is what sits between the teams: a bed that is clean but unassigned, a discharge waiting on one signature, a bill missing a consumable that was used in theatre, a claim waiting for one document, a register that is complete in one ward and not another.

Published figures show the cost of this friction in one place. A 2024 survey by a healthcare provider found five in ten patients faced discharge delays because of claim processing, and about 38 percent said discharge took over eight hours. That is one survey, but any hospital manager will recognise the pattern.

What most hospitals run on

  • Registration and billing software
  • Paper case sheets and ward registers
  • Excel for beds, stock and claims
  • WhatsApp groups between departments
  • Insurer and TPA portals and email

Each tool does its own job. Nothing joins the queue, the bed, the bill and the file, so people are the glue.

16 problems, one by one

Where a hospital loses time, money and control.

These are the situations hospital owners, administrators and department heads describe. For each one: what it looks like on an ordinary day, what it costs, and what Verity does about it.

  1. 01OPD

    The OPD queue has no shape

    What it looks like

    Forty patients are waiting outside one consultant's room, six outside another's. Tokens are handed out in the order people arrive, not the order they were booked. A patient who came for a review stands in the same line as a new case, and the front desk cannot say how long the wait is.

    What it costs you

    Example arithmetic: if a consultant sees 40 patients in a morning and the average wait is 50 minutes, that is more than 30 hours of patient time lost before noon. Long waits send patients to the next clinic.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each OPD visit is a record with a doctor, a time slot, a status (waiting, with doctor, billed, done) and a wait time. Verity shows the queue per consultant and flags the rooms where waits are running long, so the desk can rebalance.

    See the screen
  2. 02OPD

    Doctor schedules, no-shows and double-booked slots

    What it looks like

    A consultant's weekly OPD timing changes for a surgery day and the desk finds out when patients arrive. Follow-up slots are given on paper. Some patients do not turn up; others arrive for a doctor who is in theatre.

    What it costs you

    A wasted slot cannot be sold again. A patient sent away for a schedule mix-up rarely comes back.

    How Verity fixes it

    Consultant timings, leave and theatre days are on one schedule. Follow-up visits are created from the previous visit with a date and owner, and no-shows are marked so the desk can ask staff to call. Staff make the call; Verity records the outcome.

    See the screen
  3. 03Records

    The same patient has three records

    What it looks like

    A patient is registered as Ramesh Kumar at the OPD desk, R. Kumar at the lab and Ramesh Kumar Singh at admission. The old discharge summary is under one name and the new reports under another.

    What it costs you

    Duplicate files mean missing history, repeated tests and bills that go to the wrong account. They also make it harder to answer a patient or an auditor who asks for the full record.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each patient has one record with a visit history, and a possible duplicate is flagged at registration for a person to merge or keep. Reports and documents attach to that record, with permissions on who can open them.

    See the screen
  4. 04Beds

    Nobody knows which beds are really free

    What it looks like

    The ward board says bed 12 is free. Housekeeping has not cleaned it. Bed 14 is occupied but the patient left an hour ago. The emergency desk phones three wards to find a bed while a patient waits on a trolley.

    What it costs you

    An idle bed is lost revenue and a blocked bed is a delayed admission. One ICRA sample put private hospital occupancy at around 62 to 64 percent in FY2023, so every empty bed matters.

    How Verity fixes it

    Every bed has a state: occupied, discharge pending, being cleaned, ready, blocked. Housekeeping and nurses update it on the ward screen. The admission desk sees ready beds by ward and type without making calls.

    See the screen
  5. 05Beds

    Discharge takes half a day

    What it looks like

    The doctor has cleared the patient at 10 am. The final bill waits for pharmacy returns, the summary waits for a signature, the insurer waits for a document, and the family waits in the room. The bed is not ready until evening.

    What it costs you

    A survey by a healthcare provider reported that about 38 percent of respondents said discharge took over eight hours. Every extra hour is a bed that cannot take the next admission.

    How Verity fixes it

    Discharge is a checklist on the patient record with an owner for each step: summary, pharmacy clearance, final bill, insurer authorisation, medicines and instructions handed over. The screen shows which step is holding each discharge, so the right person is chased.

    See the screen
  6. 06Wards

    Ward tasks and handovers live in notebooks

    What it looks like

    The night nurse hands over verbally. A request to reassess a dressing, to arrange a transfer, to chase a report, is said once and forgotten by morning. Nurses and ward boys are rostered by memory.

    What it costs you

    Missed tasks become complaints, and an incident with no written trail is hard to explain afterwards.

    How Verity fixes it

    Ward tasks are assigned to a named person with a time, shown on the ward screen and carried across shifts. The roster shows who is on each ward, and each handover leaves a record of what was open.

    See the screen
  7. 07Billing

    Charges used in the ward never reach the bill

    What it looks like

    A consumable is used in theatre and the slip is lost. A bedside procedure is done at night and not entered. A package patient is billed for an item that the package already covered.

    What it costs you

    Example arithmetic: if 3 charges of ₹800 each go unposted on one in ten admissions, and the hospital has 600 admissions a month, that is about ₹1.4 lakh of unbilled care every month.

    How Verity fixes it

    Consumables, procedures and visits are recorded against the admission as they happen. Verity compares what was used and done with what the bill carries, and lists the gaps before the final bill is closed.

    See the screen
  8. 08Billing

    Estimates, deposits and the final bill do not match

    What it looks like

    The family is told ₹1.8 lakh at admission. The final bill is ₹2.6 lakh. Nobody warned them when the stay was extended or a procedure added. The billing desk now has an argument at the counter.

    What it costs you

    Disputes slow discharge, hurt trust and lead to waivers given in a hurry.

    How Verity fixes it

    The estimate, deposits and running charges sit on the admission record. When the running total passes the estimate by a set share, Verity alerts the billing desk and the ward so the family is told early. Staff speak to the family.

    See the screen
  9. 09Insurance

    Insurance pre-approvals and queries are chased by phone

    What it looks like

    The insurance desk has forty open pre-authorisations. One insurer asked for a document two days ago and nobody saw the email. Another approved a lower amount than requested and the ward does not know.

    What it costs you

    Insurers are generally required to decide cashless requests within an hour and give final authorisation on discharge within three hours of the hospital's request, so a delay at the hospital's end, such as a missing document, is a delay the hospital owns.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each insurer case is a record with status, amount asked, amount approved, documents sent and documents requested. Verity lists cases that wait on the hospital and cases that wait on the insurer, with age. The desk sends the documents; Verity tracks who owes what.

    See the screen
  10. 10Insurance

    Claims come back short and money is stuck with TPAs

    What it looks like

    A claim is paid ₹62,000 against ₹78,000 billed, with a deduction note nobody reads. Old claims sit unpaid for months. The accounts team cannot say which insurer owes how much.

    What it costs you

    Example arithmetic: ₹40 lakh stuck with insurers for 60 days instead of 30 is working capital the hospital finances itself, on top of the deductions that are never challenged.

    How Verity fixes it

    Billed, approved, settled and deducted amounts are on each claim, with the deduction reason. Verity shows outstanding by insurer and age, and flags claims short-paid beyond a limit so someone raises the query.

    See the screen
  11. 11Stock

    Pharmacy and ward stores run out or expire

    What it looks like

    The ward store runs out of IV sets at 2 am. The central pharmacy holds a batch of an injection that expires in three weeks. Theatre implants are kept on consignment and the count in the register does not match the count in the cupboard.

    What it costs you

    Emergency purchases cost more than planned ones, and expired stock is paid-for stock thrown away. Implants that are used but not billed are a direct loss.

    How Verity fixes it

    Items are tracked by batch and expiry across the central store, pharmacy and each ward store. Consumption by ward drives reorder points, near-expiry lists go to the pharmacist, and implants are recorded to the patient when used.

    See the screen
  12. 12Suppliers

    Purchases, suppliers and price creep

    What it looks like

    Three departments order from the same supplier at three different rates. Goods arrive short and the invoice is passed anyway. A rate contract expired last month and nobody noticed.

    What it costs you

    Overpaying on high-volume items quietly compounds, and paying for short deliveries is pure loss.

    How Verity fixes it

    Purchase orders, receipts and invoices are matched, and rates are compared across departments and suppliers. A rate that rises beyond your limit, or a delivery that does not match the order, is flagged before payment.

    See the screen
  13. 13Equipment

    Biomedical equipment is serviced when it breaks

    What it looks like

    The ventilator was due for service in August. The theatre light has no service record. The vendor's number is in the biomedical engineer's phone, and he is on leave.

    What it costs you

    Equipment that fails during use is a patient safety event, and an unplanned repair costs more than a booked one.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each device is an asset record with service history, calibration dates and the vendor. Verity raises tasks before a date is due, and keeps downtime and repair history on the record.

    See the screen
  14. 14NABH and records

    Files are incomplete when the auditor asks

    What it looks like

    Two weeks before an accreditation visit, the quality team checks 200 closed files. Consent forms are unsigned in some, the discharge summary is missing in others, and nursing notes are not countersigned.

    What it costs you

    Gaps found at the audit are findings, and rushing to fix files in the last fortnight takes clinicians away from patients. The 6th edition of the NABH hospital standards applies to new applications from 1 January 2025 and puts weight on digital information management.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each admission has a completeness checklist: consent, summary, notes, orders, billing sign-off. Verity shows missing items by file, ward and doctor each week, so files are completed while the patient episode is fresh.

    See the screen
  15. 15Compliance

    Licences, staff credentials and who may see which record

    What it looks like

    A nurse's registration renewal lapses. The fire safety certificate date is in a file. Interns and billing staff can open any patient's file. Nobody can say who looked at a particular celebrity's record.

    What it costs you

    Lapsed credentials are a compliance gap, and open access to health data is a legal and reputational risk. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Rules were notified on 14 November 2025, with most duties applying 18 months later.

    How Verity fixes it

    Staff credentials, licences and certificates are records with expiry and owner. Access to patient records is set by role, and every view or change leaves an audit trail.

    See the screen
  16. 16Owner

    Consultant payouts and the owner's blind spot

    What it looks like

    Visiting consultants are paid a share of their billing. The calculation is done by hand at month end and disputed. The owner asks what the hospital earned from orthopaedics last quarter and waits three days for a sheet.

    What it costs you

    Disputes strain relations with consultants, and decisions about which department to grow wait for slow numbers. A second branch doubles the problem.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each bill is tagged to the consultant and department, so shares and department income come from the same records. Branches are locations that roll up, with occupancy, waits, claims outstanding and stock side by side.

How we work with you

We set it up with you, in four weeks.

You do not get a login and a manual. We spend time in your OPD, wards, billing office and stores, build Verity around how your hospital really runs, bring your data in, then stay to make sure it keeps working.

  1. Week 1

    Discovery and mapping

    What we do
    Walk one patient journey from registration to discharge with your front desk, ward nurses, billing and insurance desks. Sit with the pharmacy, stores and quality coordinator. Note where things get stuck.
    What we need from you
    Three to four hours of yours and your administrator's. A department head per area for an hour each. Access to sample bills, claim files and stock registers.
    What you have at the end
    A written map of your hospital: departments, wards and bed types, consultants, insurers, stores and the first problems we will close.

  2. Weeks 2 to 3

    Configuration

    What we do
    Set up departments, wards, beds, consultants, schedules, discharge steps, claim statuses, store locations, reorder points, equipment and the six screens in your own terms. Set who may see what.
    What we need from you
    Check each area with its head. Tell us where a step, a form name or a rule is wrong. Agree access by role with your IT or compliance lead.
    What you have at the end
    A working Verity set up with your wards, doctors and rules, ready for your data.

  3. Weeks 3 to 4

    Migration and training

    What we do
    Bring in your patient list, open admissions, open claims, stock and supplier lists, equipment and staff credentials from the sheets and systems you use. Train each team at its desk during a real shift.
    What we need from you
    Extracts from your current systems and an opening stock count. An hour with each role.
    What you have at the end
    Your live data inside Verity and teams that have used it through real shifts.

  4. After go-live

    Operations partnership

    What we do
    Stay on after launch. Review the first weeks of waits, discharge times and claim ageing with you, tune the limits, and handle changes when you add a ward, a department or a branch.
    What we need from you
    A short monthly review with the administrator and finance head.
    What you have at the end
    A system that keeps matching how the hospital runs, with someone responsible for it.

Rules and paperwork

The rules a hospital has to keep up with.

Verity does not give legal or medical advice and does not file anything for you. It keeps the records these rules depend on in order, so you are not hunting for them when someone asks.

  • Clinical establishment registration

    What is expected

    Hospitals generally need registration under the Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010 where a state has adopted it, or under the state's own nursing home or establishment law elsewhere. Rules and renewals differ by state.

    How Verity helps

    The registration is a record with number, category, dates and an owner, and changes to beds or services are tasks.

  • NABH accreditation standards

    What is expected

    Hospitals that seek or hold NABH accreditation work to the standards. The 6th edition applies to new applications from 1 January 2025, with attention to information management and digital health.

    How Verity helps

    Completeness checklists per admission and sampled by ward, with dates for equipment and credential items.

    Digital Health News

  • Health data and the DPDP Rules, 2025

    What is expected

    Hospitals handle health data, treated as high risk. The Rules were notified on 14 November 2025 and most duties apply 18 months later. They include security safeguards and keeping logs for at least a year. Erasure requests can generally be declined where clinical record laws require retention.

    How Verity helps

    Role-based access and an audit trail on every view and change.

    SCC Online

  • Biomedical waste, fire safety and local permissions

    What is expected

    Hospitals generally need biomedical waste authorisation and fire safety certification, and local permits, with dates that vary by state.

    How Verity helps

    Each is a record with the issuing body, date and owner, with a task before it lapses.

Rules change and differ by state. Confirm current requirements with your legal adviser, your NABH consultant and the relevant authority.

A billing clerk should not read a clinical note. A ward nurse should not see consultant payouts. Each role opens on what it needs.

Questions

Questions hospital owners ask.

Does Verity replace our hospital information system?

No. Whatever runs your registration and billing keeps doing so. We agree during setup how patient and bill data reach Verity, which then connects the queue, beds, claims, stores and files around them.

Will it store treatment decisions or give clinical advice?

No. Verity records and organises: visits, bed states, charges, documents and tasks. It does not advise on diagnosis or treatment. Clinical notes stay under your doctors' control and your clinical record rules.

How do you handle patient privacy?

Access is set by role, so billing staff, ward staff and doctors each see only what they need. Every view and change is recorded with who and when. We agree the access plan with your compliance lead in week 2.

Can it help with NABH preparation?

It keeps the records NABH reviewers ask for in order: file completeness, equipment service, credentials and licences. It does not certify you or file anything. Your NABH consultant still advises on the standards.

Can it submit claims to insurers?

No. Your insurance desk submits through the insurer or TPA portals. Verity tracks each case, the documents sent and requested, what was approved and what was paid.

We have two branches. Can we see them together?

Yes. Each is a location with beds, waits, claims and stock side by side, and patients can be one record across both.

Will doctors and nurses accept more data entry?

Only if it replaces work. We keep entries to a tap or two at the point of work, train at the desk during real shifts, and start with the few records that matter most.

How quickly will we see something useful?

Bed states, discharge checklists and claim ageing need only your existing data, so we aim to have them first. Waits and consumption patterns become reliable after a few weeks of recorded activity.

What does it cost?

It depends on the number of branches, beds, users and what you want set up. Message us and we will give you a clear number after a short conversation about how your hospital runs.
