How the business runs

A factory is a chain, and the chain is only as visible as its worst-recorded link.

Manufacturing is a sequence of dependent transitions. Raw material arrives against a purchase order, is inspected and taken into stock, is issued to production, becomes work in progress, passes or fails quality, becomes finished goods, is committed to a customer order and leaves through dispatch. Every one of those transitions changes the state of something valuable, and every one of them is a place where the record can be lost.

When those transitions live in different systems — or in different registers, which is more common — the plant can see that an order is late but cannot see why. The customer service team knows the shipment did not go. Dispatch knows it did not have the goods. Production knows the batch was held. Quality knows it failed a second inspection. Purchasing knows the substitute material arrived four days late. Nobody has all five facts at once, which is why the daily production meeting exists.

The second cost is planning. Committing to a delivery date requires knowing what material is actually available, what is already committed to other orders, and what the line can produce in the intervening time. In most factories that calculation is done from memory and optimism, which is why commitments slip.

Verity is built on exactly this shape. Materials, work, suppliers, quality states, locations and dispatch are records in one system with one history, so the QC hold that is blocking three dispatches appears in the same view as the dispatches it is blocking.

What gets in the way

Every delay has a cause. Most causes are unrecorded.

The problems in a factory are not mysterious. They are simply not written down at the point they occur, so they surface later as something else.

  • Late orders have untraceable causes

    The delay is visible at dispatch. The cause was a quality hold, a material shortage or a machine stoppage several days earlier, and connecting the two takes a meeting.

    In Verity Each transition is a state change on a record, so a delayed order carries the chain of states that produced it.

  • Material availability is not the same as material stock

    A store shows six tonnes. Four are already committed to confirmed orders, and nobody knows that until production stops.

    In Verity Stock committed to orders is distinguished from stock available, so a delivery commitment is made against what is genuinely free.

  • Quality holds are communicated informally

    A batch is held on the shop floor and dispatch finds out when the truck is loading.

    In Verity A hold is a state on the batch record, and the orders depending on that batch show as blocked at the moment it is applied.

  • Work issued to job workers disappears

    Material sent out for plating, machining or finishing leaves the plant and exists only in a challan book until it comes back.

    In Verity Material issued to a job worker is recorded movement against that supplier, so what is outside the plant is visible and ageing.

  • Supplier reliability is anecdotal

    Everyone knows which vendors are difficult. Nobody can say by how much, or what it has cost in delayed orders.

    In Verity Ordered dates against received dates are recorded on every purchase, so reliability is a number attached to a supplier.

  • The daily meeting is the reporting system

    Production status is assembled verbally each morning because there is no view that assembles itself.

    In Verity A live operational picture removes the need to reconstruct yesterday before the day can start.

The complete system

Everything Verity manages for manufacturers

This is the operation Verity’s record model was shaped around. These are the parts a manufacturer works with.

Inventory

Raw material, work in progress and finished goods

Stock is held with its supplier, cost, batch, location and state, and distinguishes what is available from what is already committed.

Why it matters here A factory has at least three inventories that most systems treat as one. Committing a delivery date against the wrong one is how promises break.

In practice Six tonnes in the store, four committed to confirmed orders, two genuinely available. The delivery commitment is made against the two.

Work

Work orders, batches and job cards

Production work is held as records with an owner, a line, a state and a history, connected to the material it consumes and the order it fulfils.

Why it matters here The work order is the link between a customer commitment and the material that will satisfy it. Without it, planning is guesswork.

In practice Batch 214 is a record connecting the raw material issued, the line that ran it, the inspection it is held on and the three dispatches waiting on it.

Workflows

Inspections, holds and approvals

Quality checks, holds, deviations, purchase approvals and dispatch clearances move through defined steps with an owner and a recorded decision.

Why it matters here Quality is the step most likely to be handled by conversation, and it is the step that most often blocks everything downstream.

In practice A second inspection failure holds the batch and immediately marks the dependent orders as blocked, with the reason attached.

Orders

Customer orders and commitments

Orders are records with their items, quantities, committed dates, production state and dispatch history.

Why it matters here The committed date is the promise the whole factory is organised around, and it should be visible against the work that will meet it.

In practice Fourteen orders past their committed date, each showing the step it is stuck at rather than just the number of days lost.

Suppliers

Vendors, job workers and transporters

Suppliers are relationships with purchase orders, delivery performance, material issued to them and outstanding balances.

Why it matters here Inbound reliability determines outbound reliability. A factory that cannot measure its suppliers is absorbing their variance.

In practice A vendor’s average delay of four days is visible against the orders it affected, which changes the conversation at the next negotiation.

Logistics

Dispatch, vehicles and delivery

Dispatch is tracked against the orders it fulfils, with vehicles, routes and delivery confirmation on the record.

Why it matters here A dispatch that leaves without documentation, or a delivery nobody confirmed, becomes a receivables problem two months later.

In practice Three vehicles queued at the loading bay, each against specific orders, with the goods either cleared or held.

Locations

Plants, lines, stores and warehouses

Locations roll into organisations and organisations into the business, with permissions, reporting and exceptions following the same structure.

Why it matters here Multi-plant manufacturers cannot compare anything unless every plant records it the same way.

In practice Throughput, exceptions and on-time performance by plant, from the same records rather than from four differently formatted reports.

People

Operators, supervisors and shifts

Teams, roles and responsibilities are modelled once, and every work record shows who owns it and who last acted on it.

Why it matters here When a step stalls, the useful question is who owns the next action. That should not require asking around.

In practice A held batch shows the supervisor responsible for the next decision, and the escalation path if it is not taken.

Workforce

Assignment, attendance and availability

Who is assigned, who is present and what they completed stay connected to the work they support.

Why it matters here A line short two operators produces less, and that connection should be visible in the production numbers rather than inferred.

In practice Attendance against shift plan on Line 3 sits alongside the output that shift actually produced.

Relationships

Customers behind the orders

Customers are records with their orders, agreed terms, delivery history, quality history and outstanding balances.

Why it matters here A factory that only holds orders can see what is late. A factory that holds customers can see which relationship has absorbed three late deliveries in a row and is about to be lost.

In practice An account whose last four orders all shipped past their committed date is visible as a relationship at risk, not just as four separate exceptions.

Records

Specifications, drawings and test reports

Documents attach to the product, batch or order they belong to, with version history and the same permission model as everything else.

Why it matters here The specification a batch was made against, and the report that cleared it, are needed years later during a complaint or an audit, usually by someone who was not there.

In practice A customer queries a batch shipped eighteen months ago. The drawing revision, the inspection report and the release decision are on the batch record.

Control

Traceability, permissions and audit

One permission model and one audit trail across every record, so every state change carries the person who made it and when.

Why it matters here Traceability is not a reporting feature in manufacturing, it is the ability to answer what went into a batch and who released it. That has to be a property of the records themselves.

In practice A recall enquiry resolves to the material lots consumed, the line that ran them and the person who cleared the batch, from the trail rather than from a reconstruction.

Communication

Shop-floor context on the record

Comments, notifications and activity attach to the work order, batch or delivery they concern.

Why it matters here Most of what a factory knows about why something went wrong is said out loud at a handover and never written down.

In practice The note that a substitute material was used on a batch sits on that batch, where quality will see it rather than hear about it.

Command centre

The plant as it is running

One live view of what is moving, what is blocked, who owns it and what needs attention today.

Why it matters here The morning production meeting exists because no such view exists. Most of that meeting is reconstruction rather than decision.

In practice Orders in flight, orders past date, batches held, material coverage and approvals pending, in one picture that assembles itself.

Reports and analytics

Reports from the production records

Throughput, on-time delivery, rejection rates, material consumption, supplier reliability and order ageing come from the operational records themselves.

Why it matters here Manufacturing reporting is usually a monthly compilation, which is far too slow for decisions taken daily.

In practice Rolling seven-day throughput and exception closure rates, current rather than compiled.

Work in motion

The chain, recorded transition by transition.

These sequences already run in your plant. In Verity each step is a state change on a record, so a break in the chain is visible where it happens.

Purchase to material availability

  1. 01 Purchase order raised against a supplier with a required-by date
  2. 02 Goods received and recorded against the order
  3. 03 Incoming inspection completed, accepted or rejected
  4. 04 Accepted material taken into stock with batch and location
  5. 05 Supplier delivery performance updated on the record
  6. 06 Material availability recalculated against committed orders

Rejection at incoming inspection is recorded against the supplier, not absorbed as an ordinary shortage.

Customer order to dispatch

  1. 01 Order confirmed with quantities and a committed date
  2. 02 Material availability checked against existing commitments
  3. 03 Work order raised and assigned to a line
  4. 04 Production completed and moved to inspection
  5. 05 Quality passed and goods taken into finished stock
  6. 06 Order picked, documented and dispatched
  7. 07 Delivery confirmed and the order closed

Every stage carries a timestamp, so cycle time is measured rather than estimated.

Quality hold and release

  1. 01 Inspection records a failure against the batch
  2. 02 Batch state moves to held; dependent orders show as blocked
  3. 03 Deviation or rework decision routed for approval
  4. 04 Rework completed and re-inspected
  5. 05 Batch released or scrapped, with the outcome recorded
  6. 06 Dependent orders unblocked and dispatch resumes

The three dispatches waiting on a held batch know they are waiting the moment the hold is applied.

Job work sent outside the plant

  1. 01 Material issued to the job worker and recorded as movement
  2. 02 Expected return date set on the record
  3. 03 Ageing tracked while the material is outside the plant
  4. 04 Returned quantity received and reconciled against what was issued
  5. 05 Losses or shortfalls recorded against the job worker

Material outside the plant stops being a challan in a book and becomes stock with a location and an age.

Shortage and expediting

  1. 01 Material coverage falls below the horizon for confirmed orders
  2. 02 Shortage flagged against the orders it will affect
  3. 03 Purchase raised or expedited with the supplier
  4. 04 Approval applied where the price or urgency requires it
  5. 05 Affected order commitments reviewed and communicated

The shortage is connected to the customer orders it endangers, so the commercial consequence is visible with the operational one.

Monthly performance review

  1. 01 On-time delivery pulled against committed dates
  2. 02 Rejection and rework rates pulled by line and product
  3. 03 Supplier delivery performance compiled from purchase records
  4. 04 Exception causes grouped by type and frequency
  5. 05 Actions raised against the recurring causes with owners

The review works from records rather than from a compilation exercise that takes three days to prepare.

Questions

Questions manufacturers ask

What can AI software do for a manufacturing business?

Verity AI answers questions from your own production, material, quality and order records. You can ask which orders are at risk and what is blocking each one, what is held at quality, which materials fall short of cover, or which suppliers delivered late — and turn the answer into follow-ups assigned to the people who own the blocking steps.

Is Verity an ERP?

Verity is an operational layer rather than a replacement for statutory accounting. It holds the material, production, quality, supplier, dispatch and order records that run the plant, and it is introduced alongside an existing ERP rather than requiring it to be switched off.

Can Verity track material issued to job workers?

Yes. Material issued outside the plant is recorded as movement against that job worker with an expected return date, so what is out, how long it has been out and what came back is visible without a challan book.

Does it distinguish available stock from committed stock?

Yes, and that distinction is the point. Stock committed to confirmed orders is separated from stock genuinely available, so delivery commitments are made against material that is actually free.

How does it handle quality holds?

A hold is a state on the batch record. The moment it is applied, the customer orders depending on that batch show as blocked with the reason attached, so dispatch and customer service know at the same time as the shop floor.

Can it work across multiple plants?

Yes. Plants, lines, stores and warehouses are locations that roll into organisations and into the business, with permissions and reporting following the same structure, so throughput and on-time performance are comparable across sites.

Can we measure supplier reliability?

Ordered dates against received dates and incoming inspection outcomes are recorded on every purchase, so delivery performance and incoming quality become numbers attached to each supplier rather than reputations.

Will this replace our daily production meeting?

It replaces the reconstruction part of it. The live operational picture assembles what is moving, what is blocked and who owns it, so the meeting can be about decisions rather than about establishing yesterday’s facts.

How long does implementation take?

About four weeks: discovery and mapping of how the plant actually runs, configuration, migration of your existing records, then an ongoing operations partnership rather than a handover and a manual.
