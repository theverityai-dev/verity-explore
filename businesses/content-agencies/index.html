How it runs today

The writing is the easy part. The traffic around it is not.

A content agency of ten to forty people runs a calendar for each client: blogs, social posts, newsletters, scripts, case studies. Each piece moves from brief to draft to edit to client review to publish. The writers are a mix of in-house staff and freelancers.

The system for this is usually a shared sheet, a task board and a lot of chat. The calendar is in one place, the drafts in another, the client's comments in email, the freelancers' invoices in a third and the signed agreements, if any, nowhere. The editor who holds it together answers most questions from memory.

So the agency finds out late. A piece that went through five rounds on a fee that allowed two. A freelancer who missed a deadline and nobody knew until publish day. A client calendar with eight pieces due on one Friday. A deliverable the client now wants to use in print, with no written record of what rights were given.

What most content agencies run on

  • Content calendar in a shared sheet
  • Task board for drafts
  • Documents shared by link
  • Freelancer chat groups
  • Invoice and payout sheet

Each tool is good enough on its own. Nothing connects the client, the piece, the rounds, the writer and the payment.

13 problems, one by one

Where a content agency actually loses money.

These are the situations content heads, editors and agency owners describe again and again. For each one: what it looks like, what it costs, and what Verity does about it.

  1. 01Calendar

    The calendar bunches up on Fridays

    What it looks like

    A client's monthly plan has twelve pieces. Eight fall due in the last week because the client's launch is on the 30th. The same editor reviews all of them on the 28th.

    What it costs you

    Late nights, rushed edits and a higher error rate in the pieces that matter most.

    How Verity fixes it

    The calendar shows pieces by client, due date and stage, and the editor's load by week. A week that holds more than a person can edit is flagged while the plan is being made, and pieces can be moved with a reason.

  2. 02Calendar

    A thin brief becomes a bad first draft

    What it looks like

    The brief says "write a blog on cyber safety, 800 words." No audience, keyword, tone, source, call to action or example is given. The draft misses on all six.

    What it costs you

    Example arithmetic: a ₹6,000 article that needs three extra rounds at two hours each is 6 extra hours, about ₹4,000 of editor time.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each piece starts as a brief with audience, goal, keyword, tone, sources, length and call to action. A brief missing the required fields cannot be assigned to a writer, and the client's earlier approved briefs are on the record.

    See the screen
  3. 03Revisions

    Revision rounds nobody counted

    What it looks like

    A retainer includes two rounds per piece. Client comments arrive in pieces over a week by email, chat and a shared document. By the sixth round no one can say how many there have been.

    What it costs you

    Rounds are the largest hidden cost of content work. Example arithmetic: 12 pieces a month with two extra rounds each at 90 minutes a round is 36 hours, close to a week of one person.

    How Verity fixes it

    Every draft has a round number, the date it went to the client and the date feedback arrived. Comments are attached to the round. Verity shows rounds used against rounds included and holds a new round for a quote when the limit is passed.

    See the screen
  4. 04Revisions

    Conflicting feedback from three client people

    What it looks like

    The marketing head wants a casual tone. The founder asks to make it formal. The legal contact deletes a paragraph. The writer rewrites to please the last voice.

    What it costs you

    Rounds multiply and the final copy reads as a compromise nobody likes.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each client record names one final approver per content type. Feedback from other people is logged as comments and the approver's decision is the one that closes the round.

    See the screen
  5. 05Revisions

    The client approves slowly, then wants it tomorrow

    What it looks like

    A draft sits with the client for nine days. When it is approved the post has to go out the next morning. The team works the night.

    What it costs you

    Rushed work and, often, errors. Writers who are asked to turn around a piece overnight are not paid for it.

    How Verity fixes it

    A draft with the client for more than a set number of days is flagged to the account manager, with the new publish date. The record shows how long each client takes to approve on average, which supports a conversation about turnaround.

    See the screen
  6. 06Freelancers

    Freelancers are found, not managed

    What it looks like

    The team has 40 freelancers across writing, design, video and translation. Their rates are in a chat. Nobody remembers who is good at fintech or who missed the last two deadlines.

    What it costs you

    Good freelancers are lost to overbooking. Poor ones are hired again because their record is nowhere.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each freelancer is a record with skills, languages, rate, past pieces, on-time delivery and quality notes. Searching for a fintech writer who can do Hindi lists who is free and how they performed.

    See the screen
  7. 07Freelancers

    A freelancer misses the deadline and no one knows

    What it looks like

    A piece is due Wednesday at noon. The freelancer says "almost done" on Tuesday and goes quiet. On Wednesday afternoon the editor starts writing it from scratch.

    What it costs you

    A day of the editor's time and a late delivery to the client.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each assignment has a draft deadline well before the publish date. If it is missed, the editor is alerted with the backup writers who are free, and the miss goes on the freelancer's record.

    See the screen
  8. 08Freelancers

    Freelancer payments and tax paperwork

    What it looks like

    Payouts are made at the end of the month from a sheet. A writer is paid for a piece that was rejected. Another is paid twice. TDS and PAN details are missing for three people.

    What it costs you

    Overpayment, disputes, and questions from your accountant. Under Section 194J, tax is generally deducted on professional fees once payments cross ₹50,000 in a year, at 10% for professional services.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each assignment has an agreed fee and a status. A payment task appears only when the piece is accepted. The freelancer's PAN, bank and year-to-date total sit on the record for the accountant, who deducts tax and pays. Verity does not run the payment.

    See the screen
  9. 09Rights

    Nobody wrote down who owns the work

    What it looks like

    A freelancer wrote an ebook. The client now wants to reuse it in print and in another country. The agency's agreement with the freelancer says nothing, and neither does the one with the client.

    What it costs you

    Under the Copyright Act, an assignment must be in writing, and if no period is stated it is generally taken as five years, and if no territory is stated, as India only. Rights not used within a year can lapse unless the assignment says otherwise. A client who assumed perpetual worldwide use can be surprised, and so can the agency.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each deliverable has a rights record: who created it, the agreement signed, the period, the territory and the client it was assigned to. A piece cannot be marked delivered without it.

    See the screen
  10. 10Rights

    Sponsored and influencer content without the right label

    What it looks like

    A brand's blog includes a paid creator's quote. A video script lists a product placement. The labelling is the writer's guess.

    What it costs you

    ASCI's 2025 scorecard found only 29% of the posts it reviewed carried adequate disclosure. A complaint lands on the brand and often the agency that produced the content.

    How Verity fixes it

    Pieces with a paid mention, gifted product or sponsored placement are tagged, and the disclosure line and its placement are on the checklist. The editor ticks it before the piece is approved for publishing.

    See the screen
  11. 11Rights

    AI-assisted content with no record of how it was made

    What it looks like

    A writer uses an AI tool for a first draft and an AI voice for a video. The client's contract says original human work. Nobody noted which tool was used.

    What it costs you

    From 20 February 2026, amendments to the IT Rules require platforms that enable synthetic content to label it and attach provenance markers, so synthetic audio and video is under scrutiny. The agency's contract and its client's rules decide what is allowed.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each piece records whether AI tools were used and which, as your client contracts require. A client with a no-AI clause shows a warning on every piece, and the editor confirms the check.

    See the screen
  12. 12Retainers

    Retainer volume: promised, delivered and billed

    What it looks like

    A client pays for 16 pieces a month. Eleven were delivered in September, 19 in October. Nobody tracked what carried over, and the invoice is for 16 every month.

    What it costs you

    Over-delivery is unpaid work, under-delivery is a dispute. Both repeat each month.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each retainer lists the pieces included by type. Delivered pieces are counted against it by month, with carry-over rules recorded, so extra work is quoted and shortfalls are caught early.

    See the screen
  13. 13Owner

    Which clients and content types make money

    What it looks like

    A long-form article takes 9 hours of work for a ₹7,000 fee. A social post takes 40 minutes for ₹1,500. Nobody has compared them.

    What it costs you

    The agency keeps selling what is easiest to sell, not what pays best.

    How Verity fixes it

    Time and freelancer costs are logged against each piece. Verity shows revenue, hours and cost by client and by content type, so you can reprice the types that lose money.

Rules and paperwork

The rules a content agency has to keep up with.

Verity does not give legal or tax advice and does not file anything for you. It keeps the records these rules depend on in order, so you are not hunting for them when someone asks.

  • Copyright assignment (Copyright Act, 1957, Section 19)

    What is expected

    An assignment must be in writing and signed. If it does not state a period, it is generally taken to be five years, and if it does not state a territory, India only. Rights not used within a year can lapse unless the assignment says otherwise.

    How Verity helps

    Each deliverable has a rights record with creator, agreement, period, territory and client.

    Advocatekhoj, bare act

  • ASCI and CCPA rules on sponsored content

    What is expected

    Any material connection, such as a payment or gifted product, generally needs a prominent disclosure. ASCI found only 29% of posts reviewed had adequate disclosure in 2025.

    How Verity helps

    Pieces with paid mentions are tagged, with the disclosure line on the checklist before approval.

    exchange4media on ASCI study

  • Synthetic media labelling (IT Rules amendment, 2026)

    What is expected

    Amendments notified on 10 February 2026 and in force from 20 February 2026 require intermediaries that enable synthetic content to label it and attach provenance markers. The duties fall on platforms, and enforcement timing was reported as phased. Your client contracts may ask more of you.

    How Verity helps

    Each piece records whether AI tools were used and which, so you can answer a client or platform.

    exchange4media

  • TDS on freelancer fees (Section 194J) and GST

    What is expected

    Tax is generally deducted on professional fees above ₹50,000 a year, at 10%, and PAN is needed. Agency fees attract GST. Your accountant decides and files.

    How Verity helps

    Each freelancer record holds PAN, bank details and year-to-date fees, and each client invoice records amount and payment state.

    ClearTax

Rules change and differ by situation. Confirm current requirements with your accountant or lawyer.

Questions

Questions content agency owners ask.

My writers hate admin. Will they use it?

Only if it takes seconds. Writers see their own assignments, the brief and the deadline, and mark the draft delivered. The round tracking and rights work belong to editors and account managers.

Does Verity publish or schedule posts?

No. Your team publishes from your own tools. Verity records the piece, the approval and the publish date.

How does it count revision rounds?

Each time a draft goes to the client it becomes a numbered round with the date and the comments attached. Rounds are compared with the number included in the retainer.

Can freelancers log in?

Yes, if you want them to, with access to only their own jobs. Access can be removed when a project ends.

Can it pay my freelancers?

No. Verity shows what is accepted, what is owed and each person's year-to-date fee for your accountant. Payment and tax deduction are done by your accountant.

Does it check for plagiarism or AI use?

No. It records whether AI tools were used, and which, as your contracts require, and holds a checklist so your editor confirms the checks you already do.

We write in several languages. Is that handled?

Yes. Languages are part of each freelancer's record and each piece, so you can search for who can write in which language.

Who can see my data?

Only the people you allow. Access is set by role and client, and every change is recorded with who made it and when.

What does it cost?

It depends on the number of people, clients and what you want set up. Message us and we will give you a clear number after a short conversation about how your agency runs.
