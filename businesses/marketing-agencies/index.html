How it runs today

The work is creative and the back office is a group chat.

A marketing agency of ten to sixty people usually has good people and a thin system. Briefs come by call, WhatsApp or email. A designer works from the last message they remember. A client replies "looks good" in a chat at 11 pm, and the post goes live at 9 am.

The facts that decide profit sit in separate places. The retainer amount is in the contract. The hours are in somebody's head or a timesheet that is filled on Friday. The approvals are in email. The monthly report is a slide deck built by hand from five dashboards. The founder is the only person who sees the whole client.

So the agency finds out late. The client who asked for "one small change" for the sixth time. The retainer that took 140 hours against 90 sold. The invoice that is 75 days old while the team still works on the next campaign. The campaign that went live with a claim the client had not cleared.

What most marketing agencies run on

  • WhatsApp groups per client
  • Email threads for approvals
  • Task board or a shared sheet
  • Dashboards from each ad platform
  • Timesheet or invoice spreadsheet

Each tool is fine for its own job. Nothing connects the client, the brief, the approval, the hours and the invoice.

Where a marketing agency actually loses money.

These are the situations agency owners and account heads describe again and again. For each one: what it looks like, what it costs, and what Verity does about it.

  1. 01Briefs

    The brief is a voice note

    What it looks like

    A founder calls the account manager after dinner: "Do something for the Diwali sale, same as last year but better." The account manager passes it to the designer by WhatsApp. Nobody writes down the audience, budget, deadline or what success looks like.

    What it costs you

    Work built on a vague brief is redone. Example arithmetic: two redo rounds on a ₹40,000 project at ₹1,000 an hour of team cost is about 16 wasted hours, roughly ₹16,000.

    How Verity fixes it

    Every campaign starts as a brief record with goal, audience, budget, deliverables, deadline and owner. The brief must be marked complete before work is assigned, and every task links back to it.

  2. 02Briefs

    Two launches, one designer

    What it looks like

    Three clients want launches in the same week. The designer is booked on two of them without anyone noticing. On Wednesday one client is told the creative will be a day late.

    What it costs you

    A late launch costs goodwill, and the extra hours to catch up are usually unpaid overtime.

    How Verity fixes it

    Tasks carry an owner, an estimate and a date. Verity shows each person's load by week against the campaign calendar, so a clash shows when the work is planned, not on the day it is due.

    See the screen
  3. 03Approvals

    Approvals live in email and chats

    What it looks like

    A post is sent for approval on Monday. The client replies on WhatsApp to one line, by email to another, and the founder says "go ahead" on a call. The scheduled post goes out with the old line.

    What it costs you

    Wrong-version posts mean corrections, apologies and sometimes a client who takes the account elsewhere.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each deliverable has versions and an approval state: with team, with client, changes requested, approved. The approving person and date are recorded on the version, so only an approved version is released.

    See the screen
  4. 04Approvals

    "We never approved that"

    What it looks like

    A client disputes a campaign line after launch. The agency has screenshots in three chats and cannot show which version was cleared, by whom, or when.

    What it costs you

    Rework done for free, and a weaker position when the invoice is questioned.

    How Verity fixes it

    The approval history is part of the record: who approved which version, with comments and time. When a dispute comes, you open the deliverable and the trail is there.

    See the screen
  5. 05Retainers

    The retainer that keeps growing

    What it looks like

    A ₹1.2 lakh monthly retainer was agreed for twelve posts, one report and a monthly call. By month four it includes stories, two reels, a newsletter and weekend replies to comments.

    What it costs you

    Example arithmetic: if the team spends 130 hours on a retainer sold for 80, and an hour costs ₹900, the agency absorbs about ₹45,000 a month. A trade opinion piece describes the same pattern of retainers staying fixed while scope grows (PRmoment, 2024).

    How Verity fixes it

    Each retainer lists what is included. Work logged against it shows hours used against hours sold, and anything outside the list is marked as extra with an approval step before it is done.

    See the screen

    Nobody knows which clients make money

    What it looks like

    Revenue by client is easy. Cost by client is not. Time is logged on Friday from memory, shared across clients, and the founder's own time is not logged at all.

    What it costs you

    You keep working hard for the accounts that lose money and discount the ones that pay well.

    How Verity fixes it

    Time is logged against the client and campaign as work happens. Verity shows revenue, hours and cost per client, so the least profitable accounts are visible and can be repriced or let go.

    Monthly reports built by hand

    What it looks like

    On the 3rd of every month, an executive opens five platform dashboards, copies numbers into a deck and writes a summary. It takes most of a day per client, and the client reads one page.

    What it costs you

    Example arithmetic: 12 clients at 6 hours each is 72 hours a month, close to two weeks of one person.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each report is a recurring task with a due date, an owner and a checklist. Numbers entered or brought in for the month sit on the client record, so last month's report is the starting point, and late reports show on the owner's screen.

    Client logins and ad accounts sit with one person

    What it looks like

    The page admin rights, the ad account and the domain login for a client are held by one executive. She resigns on a Friday. On Monday nobody can pause a running ad.

    What it costs you

    Running spend nobody can stop, and a handover scramble with the client watching.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each client record lists the accounts you manage, who holds admin access, and when it was last reviewed. A departing person triggers a handover task for every account they own.

    Influencer posts without the right disclosure

    What it looks like

    A creator posts a paid reel with the disclosure buried in hashtags. The brand's name is on it, and so is the agency's.

    What it costs you

    ASCI's scorecard found 69 of the top 100 influencers failed its guidelines, so the risk is common. A takedown request or notice lands on the brand and the agency.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each creator deliverable has a checklist that includes the disclosure, the claims cleared with the client, and the date of posting. The task is not closed until a reviewer ticks it and the live link is recorded.

  11. 11Compliance

    Client data in personal phones and sheets

    What it looks like

    A customer list from a client's loyalty scheme is shared by email, saved on three laptops and used in an ad audience. When the client leaves, nobody deletes it.

    What it costs you

    New data protection rules put duties on the businesses and the processors that handle personal data, with most duties starting about 18 months after the November 2025 notification. Stray copies of client data become a risk for you and the client.

    How Verity fixes it

    Client files are records with permissions and an audit trail, so you can show who opened what. Retention dates are set per client and a task appears when a contract ends and data should be returned or deleted.

  12. 12Money

    Invoices raised late, paid later

    What it looks like

    The retainer invoice goes out on the 12th instead of the 1st. The client's finance team pays in 75 days. The agency pays salaries and freelancers on the 5th.

    What it costs you

    Agency executives have described payment cycles of 60 to 90 days after billing, so the agency lends the client money. Example arithmetic: ₹20 lakh a month in billing paid at 75 days means about ₹50 lakh is always owed to you.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each retainer has a billing date and an invoice status on the client record. Verity shows what is due, raised and overdue by client, and creates a follow-up task for the account owner. Your accountant raises the invoice; Verity makes sure it is raised on time.

    A client is about to leave and nobody saw it

    What it looks like

    Replies slow down, the monthly call gets cancelled twice, and a junior contact stops copying the head of marketing. The notice arrives anyway.

    What it costs you

    Winning a new client costs far more time than keeping one. Losing a ₹1.5 lakh retainer is ₹18 lakh a year gone.

    How Verity fixes it

    Each client shows its last meeting, open issues, approvals waiting and report status. Clients with a pattern of slow replies, missed reviews or open complaints show on a watch list with an owner and a next step.

    Freelancers, vendors and payouts in a scramble

    What it looks like

    A photographer, a video editor and two writers are hired for one campaign. Their rates are in chats, their invoices come late, and it is not clear which campaign each was for.

    What it costs you

    Overpaying, paying twice or discovering the cost after the client invoice has gone out.

    How Verity fixes it

    Vendors are records with rates, work assigned and invoices received against the campaign. The campaign shows its real cost to date, before the client invoice is raised.

Rules and paperwork

The rules a marketing agency has to keep up with.

Verity does not give legal or tax advice and does not file anything for you. It keeps the records these rules depend on in order, so you are not hunting for them when someone asks.

  • ASCI and CCPA rules on influencer and endorsement posts

    What is expected

    ASCI's influencer guidelines and the CCPA's 2022 misleading advertisement guidelines generally require clear disclosure of any material connection between an influencer and a brand, and due diligence before an endorsement. The CCPA guidelines name advertisers and agencies.

    How Verity helps

    Creator deliverables carry a checklist for disclosure and claims, with a reviewer and the live link recorded.

    Khaitan & Co on CCPA guidelines

  • Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025

    What is expected

    The Rules were notified on 14 November 2025. Most duties, including notice, security safeguards, breach reporting and retention, generally start 18 months after publication, and they cover data fiduciaries and processors. An agency handling a client's customer data may be a processor.

    How Verity helps

    Client data is stored as records with permissions and an audit trail, retention dates are set per client, and a deletion or return task is created when a contract ends.

    Scconline on DPDP Rules

  • Written contracts and scope

    What is expected

    Your client contract generally decides what the retainer includes, how changes are charged and who owns the work. Verity does not draft contracts.

    How Verity helps

    The retainer record lists inclusions and the contract date, and extra work needs an approval step so the paper matches the practice.

  • GST and TDS on agency fees

    What is expected

    Agency services generally attract GST and clients may deduct TDS on fees. Treatment depends on whether you act as principal or agent on media. Your accountant decides and files.

    How Verity helps

    Each invoice record shows the client, amount, billing date and payment state, so your accountant has clean records.

Rules change and differ by situation. Confirm current requirements with your accountant or lawyer.

Questions

Questions agency owners ask.

My team hates timesheets. Will they actually log hours?

Only if it takes seconds. We set it up so time is logged from the task itself, with a client and campaign already filled in. We start with the clients where scope is slipping, not a form for everything.

Do clients get a login?

Only if you want them to. By default your team uses Verity and the client keeps replying the way they do now. The approval is recorded by your team against the client's name and date. A client view can be agreed during setup.

Does Verity send the reports or approvals to clients?

No. Your team sends them. Verity assigns the task, shows when it is late and records the outcome.

We already use a task board. Why add this?

A task board knows tasks. It does not know the client's retainer, the approval trail or the hours against what was sold. We agree during setup how existing tasks reach Verity, or you move them.

Can it handle campaigns with many freelancers?

Yes. Freelancers and vendors are records with rates, assigned work and invoices received, and they see only their own tasks.

Can I see profit per client?

Yes, once hours and retainer terms are in. Verity shows revenue, hours, team cost and margin per client. Team cost uses the rates you set.

Does it work for small agencies of five or six people?

Yes. A small team has the same scope and approval problems with fewer people to notice them. We set up only what you need in the same four weeks.

Who can see my data?

Only the people you allow. Access is set by role and by client, and every change is recorded with who made it and when.

What does it cost?

It depends on the number of people, clients and what you want set up. Message us and we will give you a clear number after a short conversation about how your agency runs.
