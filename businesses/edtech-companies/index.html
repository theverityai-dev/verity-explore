How the business runs

Content is the cost and completion is the product.

An EdTech business spends heavily up front to produce a course and then earns from learners who enrol in it. Enrolment is the number that gets reported and completion is the number that determines renewal, referral and institutional confidence — and the gap between them is enormous. A completion rate spanning eight to seventy-four percent across courses on the same platform is a content problem, not a platform one.

That makes course-level economics the central question: what a course cost to produce, how many learners it attracted, how many finished, and what support burden it generated. Most EdTech companies measure the first two.

The second characteristic is content production, which behaves like any other project business — briefs, subject experts, production, review and release dates that slip against marketing commitments already made.

The third is support load, which concentrates on specific courses and is almost always a symptom of the content rather than of the learners.

The fourth is institutional contracts, where the buyer is an organisation with completion expectations and renewal dates, and evidence is required.

Verity records production cost and progress, completion by course and cohort, support load by course and institutional delivery.

What gets in the way

Enrolment is easy to count and completion is what matters.

EdTech difficulties come from production cost committed ahead of demand and completion nobody attributes.

  • Completion varies enormously and is not attributed

    One course completes at eight percent and another at seventy-four on the same platform, and the difference is treated as learner behaviour.

    In Verity Completion is measured per course and cohort against production cost and content structure.

  • Production slips against marketing commitments

    A course release date is committed publicly and the production runs late.

    In Verity Production is work with stages, owners and a release date, so slippage is visible before the commitment is broken.

  • Support load reveals content problems

    Tickets concentrate on specific courses and are handled as support rather than as content feedback.

    In Verity Support is recorded against the course and module, so concentration becomes a content decision.

  • Course economics are never assembled

    Production cost, enrolment, completion and support burden exist in four places.

    In Verity All four attach to the course, so contribution per course is calculable.

  • Institutional renewals need evidence

    An organisation renews on completion and outcome evidence that is assembled by hand each time.

    In Verity Cohort completion and progress are records, so renewal evidence is an extract.

  • Content ages without review

    Courses remain live long after their material has dated, and nobody owns the review.

    In Verity Courses carry review dates and performance, so refresh decisions are prompted.

Questions

Questions EdTech teams ask

Does Verity replace our learning platform?

No. The platform, content delivery and learner experience continue and are mapped during implementation. Verity records the company around them — production cost, course economics, completion, support load and institutional contracts.

What can AI software do for an EdTech company?

Verity AI answers questions from your own course, production, learner and support records: which courses have the worst completion and the highest support load, which are past their release date, what contribution looks like per course, where learners drop out. Each answer can become a content review.

Why focus on completion rather than enrolment?

Because enrolment is the number that gets reported and completion is the number that renews, refers and satisfies institutional buyers. A completion range from eight to seventy-four percent across courses on one platform is a content signal, not a learner one.

How does it handle content production?

Production is work with stages, subject experts, producers, cost and a release date, so slippage is visible before a marketing commitment is broken and cost is recorded against the course it produced.

Why treat support as content feedback?

Because it concentrates on specific modules. A module generating disproportionate support is usually a module that needs rewriting, and treating the tickets as a support burden fixes nothing.

Can it help with institutional renewals?

Cohort completion and progress are records, so the evidence an institutional buyer wants at renewal is an extract rather than something assembled by hand each time.

Does it track content ageing?

Courses carry review dates and performance, so declining completion on ageing content prompts a refresh decision rather than remaining live indefinitely.

How long does implementation take?

About four weeks: discovery and mapping of course structures, production process and contract terms, configuration, migration of courses, cohorts and contracts, then an ongoing operations partnership.
