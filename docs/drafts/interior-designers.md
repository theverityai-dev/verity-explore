## How it runs today

**The design is shown in renders. The project is run on phone calls.**

- On the first day the client sees a 3D render and a quote. After that the project becomes a stream of calls: the carpenter needs a decision, the tile has not arrived, the electrician found a beam where the downlight should go, and the client wants the wardrobe handles changed.
- Most firms track this on a site supervisor’s phone, a WhatsApp group per project, a purchase sheet and the designer’s memory. When an owner manages eight or twelve sites at once, nobody can say with confidence which site is on time, which has an unpaid milestone, or which vendor has been paid and not delivered.
- The work still gets finished. The cost is a margin that disappears in small unpriced changes, a handover that slips by weeks, and a client who by then no longer remembers the render with affection.
- runs on: WhatsApp group per project; Excel quote and BOQ; Purchase and payment sheet; Supervisor’s notebook and phone photos; Vendor phone numbers; The owner’s memory
- stack note: Each of these is useful. None of them connects a change a client asked for to its price, its vendor and its effect on the handover date.

## Pains

Heading: Where an interior project loses money and time.
Lede: These are the situations firm owners describe about their sites. For each one: what it looks like, what it costs, and what Verity does about it.

### 1. The quote missed items, and the client finds out after demolition
- area: Quote
- scene: The quote was ₹14 lakh for a 3 BHK. After demolition it becomes clear the quote left out wiring changes, a false ceiling in the passage and branded hardware. The client thought it was all included.
- cost: Example arithmetic: ₹1.2 lakh of items left out of a ₹14 lakh quote is about 9 percent. Either the firm absorbs it or the client feels cheated, and referrals suffer either way.
- fix: The quote is a list of items by room with quantity, rate and what is included or excluded. When an item turns up on site that is not on the list, it is added as a recorded change with a price, before work on it starts.
- screen: project

### 2. Client changes pile up and are never priced
- area: Changes
- scene: The client moves the TV wall, enlarges a wardrobe and swaps the vanity. Each is a small request on a call. At the end the client believes everything was in the original quote.
- cost: Variations that are not priced are the main way an interior firm loses margin. Disputes in this trade arise most often from changes to scope and from delays.
- fix: Every change is logged with the room, what changed, the extra cost and time, and whether the client approved it. The running total of approved and pending changes sits on the project, and the client signs off on each before work starts.
- screen: project

### 3. Material choices are not finalised, so orders cannot be placed
- area: Selections
- scene: The tile is chosen but the laminate shade is still pending. The kitchen cannot be ordered until the client picks a colour. Three weeks go by because the client is waiting for her mother-in-law to visit.
- cost: The carpentry slot with the workshop slips, and every trade after it slips too. The delay then gets blamed on the firm.
- fix: Each room has a list of selections to be made, with a decision date that is worked back from the site schedule. Pending selections appear on the project with the date by which the delay starts to move the handover.
- screen: materials

### 4. Deliveries slip and the chain slips with them
- area: Vendors
- scene: The modular kitchen was promised for the 20th. The factory says the 28th. The tile fixer has been booked for the 22nd. The painter has been told to start on the 1st.
- cost: A one-week slip in one trade moves the others, and labour gangs sitting idle still have to be paid or are lost to another site. A late delivery is one of the most common causes of delayed fit-outs.
- fix: Each order has a promised date, a confirmed date and a status. Late deliveries mark the trades that depend on them, so the supervisor can re-sequence work the same day instead of finding out on arrival.
- screen: schedule

### 5. Advances to vendors are paid, and nobody follows what came back
- area: Vendors
- scene: A 50 percent advance went to the wardrobe factory. A second 25 percent was released on a phone call. The delivery is half complete. The paper trail is a bank transfer and a chat message.
- cost: Money out ahead of work, and a firm that cannot show a client or an auditor which payment was for which delivery.
- fix: Every purchase order carries its payment schedule. Payments are released against a delivery or milestone, with who approved them. The materials screen shows paid, delivered and balance for each order.
- screen: materials

### 6. Supervisors see the site, the owner sees nothing
- area: Site
- scene: Eight sites, two supervisors. A weekly summary arrives by voice note. Nobody knows if the false ceiling at the second site started on Monday or was postponed.
- cost: Problems are found when the client visits, not when the supervisor notices. By then a week is gone.
- fix: The supervisor records each day’s progress by room and trade, with photos, on the site record. The owner sees each site’s planned and actual progress, and the trades running behind.
- screen: schedule

### 7. Defects are found at handover and fixed slowly
- area: Snags
- scene: On handover day the client walks through with a phone, filming. There are 43 small items: a drawer that sticks, a paint patch, a loose switch plate. Some are fixed in a week, some are forgotten.
- cost: Final payment is held until the last snag is closed. Unclear ownership of each defect stretches that by weeks.
- fix: Snags are a checklist on the project. Each one has the room, a photo, the trade responsible and a due date. Fixed items are ticked off with a photo, and the client sees how many remain.
- screen: schedule

### 8. Client milestones are not billed on time, and advances run out
- area: Money
- scene: The agreement had four payments: booking, after civil, after carpentry, at handover. Civil finished on the 12th. The second demand went out on the 28th. Meanwhile the firm paid vendors from its own pocket.
- cost: The firm finances the client’s home. Under the Consumer Protection Act, a client can complain of deficiency in service within two years, so the firm needs a record that it met its side of each milestone.
- fix: Payment milestones are tied to site stages. When the stage is marked complete with photos, a task is created to raise the demand, and the money received is shown against what was quoted and what was spent on the project.
- screen: money
- source: (S.S. Rana, on deficiency of service under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019: https://ssrana.in/articles/deficiency-of-services-consumer-protection-act-2019/)

### 9. Nobody knows which home made money
- area: Money
- scene: A home earned ₹14 lakh and cost ₹11.8 lakh. Another earned ₹22 lakh and cost ₹21.5 lakh after repairs. This is only discovered months later when the accountant tallies the vendors.
- cost: Pricing is by habit, and the firm keeps taking the kind of project that earns the least. It also cannot say which vendors or designers cause the overruns.
- fix: Quote, changes, vendor bills and labour expenses sit on the same project. A running margin is visible during the work, and a project that spends 85 percent of its budget before reaching painting is flagged.
- screen: studio

### 10. Carpenters, electricians and painters are paid by memory
- area: Vendors
- scene: The contractor team works across three sites. Running bills arrive as photos of notebook pages. Quantities are checked against measurements in a hurry, and sometimes a gang is paid twice for the same room.
- cost: Overpayment, arguments, and good tradesmen who leave because they were paid late. Micro and small vendors can expect payment within a written term of up to 45 days under the MSMED Act, so slow payment has legal weight.
- fix: Each trade’s work is recorded by room with measured quantity and rate. A bill is checked against the recorded work and paid once, with the approver’s name. Payment ageing by vendor is visible.
- screen: materials
- source: (Taxmann, on MSMED Act section 15 and section 43B(h): https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/analysis-practical-insights-into-section-43bh-regarding-disallowance-of-delayed-payments-to-mses/)

### 11. Enquiries go cold between the site visit and the quote
- area: Clients
- scene: A couple visit the studio on Saturday. The designer takes measurements on Tuesday. The quote is sent the following Wednesday. By then they have signed with a firm that quoted in three days.
- cost: Interior buyers talk to several firms at once and often decide on speed and clarity. A slow quote is a lost home.
- fix: Each enquiry is a record with the property, budget, source and owner. Quotes promised but not sent, and sent quotes with no reply after your set interval, appear as tasks. Wins and losses are recorded with a reason.
- screen: money

### 12. Clients feel uninformed, so they call more
- area: Clients
- scene: The client asks every evening how far the work has got. The designer is tired of answering. The client feels ignored, and writes a review that says so.
- cost: Hours of designer time answering the same questions, and a review that can cost the firm its next three referrals.
- fix: Each project has a short weekly summary built from its own site record: what was done, what is next, what is waiting on the client. The designer shares it, rather than answering the same call six times.
- screen: schedule

### 13. Materials at site go missing or are over-ordered
- area: Stock
- scene: Plywood sheets, laminates and hardware sit in a flat under a watchman. A box of fittings disappears. Excess tiles are left over after the job and no one knows whose they are.
- cost: Example arithmetic: ten plywood sheets at ₹3,000 each lost or over-ordered is ₹30,000 on one site, and the same pattern repeats across sites.
- fix: Materials received at site are recorded against the project with quantity and who received them. Use is recorded by room, so leftover and missing quantities show up on the site record.
- screen: materials

### 14. The owner is also the designer, the buyer and the supervisor
- area: Owner
- scene: The founder designs, negotiates with vendors, visits sites and chases payments. When they travel, projects stall waiting for one decision.
- cost: The firm cannot grow beyond what one person can hold in their head, and the owner cannot take a week off.
- fix: Each morning Verity shows a short list of what needs a decision, with an owner for every item. Approvals go to the owner’s phone with the details, and a senior designer can cover when the founder is away.
- screen: studio

## FAQ

Heading: Questions interior firm owners ask.

- **Will my site supervisors actually use it?** The daily note takes a minute: what was done, which trade, and a photo. We train on site with their own project, and supervisors get something back: a clear list of what is due and which delivery they are waiting on.
- **Does Verity replace my 3D software or quote sheet?** Your designers keep their 3D and drawing tools. The quote becomes a structured list on the project, so changes and vendor orders attach to the same items, but you can bring your existing format across.
- **Can my clients see progress?** Clients do not need to log in. Your designer shares a short summary built from the site record, so the client sees the same facts you do without a daily call.
- **Does it order from vendors?** No. Your team places orders as it does today. Verity records each order, its promised and confirmed dates, and what has been paid, and flags the ones that are late.
- **We have only three sites at a time. Is this too much?** The leaks are the same on three sites: unpriced changes, late deliveries and slow demands. We set up only what you need, and the four weeks run the same way.
- **Can it handle commercial fit-outs as well as homes?** Yes. A project is rooms or zones, trades, orders and stages, whether it is a 2 BHK or an office floor. We set the stages and approvals to suit each kind.
- **Who can see my margins and vendor rates?** Only the people you allow. Access is set by role, so supervisors, designers, accounts and vendors each see what they need, and every change is recorded with who made it and when.
- **How long before I see something useful?** The late delivery list, unapproved changes and milestones-to-bill list need only your live sites, orders and payment plans, so they come first. Margin becomes reliable after a few weeks of recorded costs.
