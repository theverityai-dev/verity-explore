## How it runs today

**The drawings are controlled. Everything around them is on trust.**

- Inside the drawing set there is order: sheet numbers, revision clouds, a title block. Outside it, a project lives in a shared folder, a client WhatsApp group, a consultant email chain and a few site photos. Which revision went to the contractor? Did the structural engineer acknowledge the latest layout? Has the municipal query been answered?
- The principal usually holds the real status in their head: which projects are waiting on a sanction, which stage fee is due, which site visit has to happen this week. Junior architects hold their own pieces. When a person is away, or a project is handed over mid-way, the picture has to be rebuilt from emails.
- Nothing fails loudly. A late drawing issue, a missed stage invoice or an unanswered query each cost a few days, and the cost shows up as late projects, uncomfortable calls with contractors and fees collected months after the work.
- runs on: CAD and BIM files on a shared drive; Email chains with consultants; Client WhatsApp groups; Excel project and fee tracker; Site photos on phones; The principal’s memory
- stack note: Each of these does its own job. None of them knows what state a project is in.

## Pains

Heading: Where an architecture practice loses time and fees.
Lede: These are the situations principals and project architects describe. For each one: what it looks like in a normal week, what it costs, and what Verity does about it.

### 1. The brief changes after the design was approved
- area: Clients
- scene: The client approved the concept and the schematic plan. Three weeks into detailed drawings they want a bigger kitchen, an extra bedroom and a new elevation. The drawings change, the structure changes, and the original fee stays the same.
- cost: Example arithmetic: a villa fee of ₹6 lakh for a 120-hour design stage that runs to 190 hours has lost 70 hours, or about ₹70,000 at a ₹1,000 hourly cost, with no one having agreed to it.
- fix: Each project keeps its approved stages and a log of client change requests. A change after approval is recorded with the date, the extra hours it needed and a decision: charged, absorbed or declined. The project architect sees the running total on the project.
- screen: project

### 2. A stage ends and nobody bills it
- area: Fees
- scene: The schematic stage was signed off in July. The detailed drawing stage is half done. The fee instalment for the first stage was never invoiced because the principal forgot and the accounts person did not know it was signed off.
- cost: Cash arrives months after the work. The Council of Architecture’s conditions of engagement tie fees to stages of work, so a stage that is finished and unbilled is simply money not collected.
- fix: Fee instalments are tied to project stages. When a stage is marked complete and the client sign-off is recorded, a task is created for the accounts person to raise the invoice, and the stage appears on the fees screen until it is paid.
- screen: fees

### 3. The wrong revision reaches the site
- area: Drawings
- scene: The contractor builds a wall from a drawing that was revised twice. The revision was emailed to the project manager but never reached the site engineer. The wall has to be broken.
- cost: Rework paid for by someone, a dispute about who is at fault, and a project architect who cannot prove which drawing was issued to whom and when.
- fix: Every drawing issue is a record: sheet number, revision, date, who received it and for what purpose, such as for approval, for tender or for construction. The current revision is clear, and a superseded one is marked so.
- screen: drawings

### 4. Municipal sanction sits in a queue and no one chases
- area: Approvals
- scene: The plan was submitted six weeks ago. A query came back about a setback, and was answered, and then nothing. The client asks every week. Nobody can say which office has the file.
- cost: Timelines vary a lot: one law-firm guide puts small houses at 15 to 30 days where processing is online and commercial or multi-storey buildings at 6 to 12 weeks, and complex projects with several clearances far longer. Each unnoticed week of waiting is a week the client cannot build.
- fix: Each statutory approval is a record with the authority, date submitted, queries raised and answered, and the next step with an owner. Approvals waiting longer than your usual time appear on a list, and where the local rules allow deemed approval, the relevant date is shown.
- screen: approvals
- source: (Sansa Legal, building permission guide: https://www.sansalegal.com/post/how-to-apply-for-a-building-construction-permission-in-india)

### 5. Clearances from fire, airport, pollution and other departments drift
- area: Approvals
- scene: The building plan is sanctioned, but the fire NOC is with one office and the pollution board consent with another. Each needs a document that someone has to remember to collect from the structural engineer.
- cost: Projects with several clearances can run to many months. A missing clearance can hold up a start on site, or an occupancy certificate at the end.
- fix: Each project lists the clearances it needs. Each clearance has a status, an owner, the documents required and who has to supply them, so a missing structural certificate is a task for the right consultant, not a surprise.
- screen: approvals

### 6. Consultant drawings clash and nobody sees it in time
- area: Consultants
- scene: The structural engineer moved a column. The MEP consultant ran a duct through it. The clash is spotted on site when the shuttering is in place.
- cost: Redesign during construction is the most expensive kind. It also damages the architect’s name with a client who sees two consultants blaming each other.
- fix: Each consultant is recorded with their scope and the drawings they owe. A coordination list shows which consultant drawings are received, which are overdue, and which have open comments, and a coordination meeting leaves tasks with owners.
- screen: drawings

### 7. Site queries and instructions are answered on the phone
- area: Site
- scene: The contractor asks about a window detail. The project architect answers on a call and forgets to write it down. Weeks later the contractor says it was the architect’s instruction.
- cost: Variation claims and disputes, with no record of who said what. Verbal instructions are the most common start of a variation claim.
- fix: Each site query is a record with the question, the drawing it refers to, the person asked, the date and the answer. Site visit notes and photos attach to the project. Open queries older than a few days show on the site screen.
- screen: site

### 8. Running bills are certified late, or without a trail
- area: Site
- scene: The contractor submits a running bill. The architect is asked to certify the work done. The site visit is delayed, the quantities are rechecked in a hurry, and the contractor complains of delayed payment.
- cost: A strained relationship with the contractor, a client who pays late, and a certificate that cannot be defended later because the measurement basis was never saved.
- fix: Each contractor bill is recorded against the project with its measurement notes, who certified it and when. A bill waiting for the architect appears with its age so certification does not sit on a desk.
- screen: site

### 9. Developer clients need certificates on a deadline
- area: Compliance
- scene: A residential developer client needs the architect’s certificate of percentage of construction completed before drawing money from the project account. The request arrives the day before.
- cost: A rushed certificate on a legal document is a risk to the architect. A late one holds up the client’s funds and their patience.
- fix: Under RERA rules the form and timing of such certificates vary by state. Verity records each certificate request, the site visit that supports it, who signed it and the date, so the evidence behind a certificate sits with the project.
- screen: project

### 10. Architects and draftspersons are overloaded or idle by accident
- area: People
- scene: Three projects hit submission on the same Friday. The senior architect is on all three. A junior has no work this week because nobody told them what was coming.
- cost: Late submissions on one side and paid idle time on the other, plus overtime that quietly builds and then burns people out.
- fix: Staff are assigned to projects by week. A load view shows booked against available hours per person and the clash of deadlines, so work is moved a week before the Friday pile-up.
- screen: practice

### 11. You do not know which projects earn money
- area: Fees
- scene: Hours are written down on Fridays, from memory, if at all. A residential interior-heavy job takes far more drafting than its fee allows. Competition entries take a month and win nothing.
- cost: Fees are set by habit, not by what a project costs to deliver, and the practice cannot tell its profitable work from its tiring work.
- fix: Hours are logged to the project and stage. Fee, hours and consultant costs sit together, so the practice sees margin per project and per stage, and which kinds of project and client are worth taking.
- screen: practice

### 12. Clients pay late and the follow-up is awkward
- area: Money
- scene: An invoice for ₹3.5 lakh is 70 days old. The principal does not want to chase a client they will work with again. The accounts person keeps a list in a notebook.
- cost: The practice finances the client. Under the MSMED Act a micro or small supplier can generally expect payment within a written term of up to 45 days, but only if the invoice was raised on time and the terms are on paper.
- fix: Invoices carry their agreed terms. Overdue ones list by client and age with a follow-up task and an owner, and each contact and promise to pay is recorded. The principal sees only the amounts that need a personal call.
- screen: fees
- source: (Taxmann, on MSMED Act section 15 and section 43B(h): https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/analysis-practical-insights-into-section-43bh-regarding-disallowance-of-delayed-payments-to-mses/)

### 13. Enquiries, proposals and competitions are tracked loosely
- area: Business
- scene: Three enquiries came in this month through friends, a builder and a past client. Two proposals are drafted. Nobody remembers when the third was promised.
- cost: Referrals are the main source of work for most practices and they expect a quick, clear reply. A slow response loses the job to someone who answered sooner.
- fix: Each enquiry is a record with a source, an owner, the proposal sent and a next step. Proposals with no reply after your set interval become follow-up tasks. Wins and losses are recorded with a reason.
- screen: fees

### 14. Details, specifications and past project knowledge leave with people
- area: Knowledge
- scene: A good waterproofing detail was drawn for a school project five years ago. A new project needs one. Nobody remembers who drew it or where the file is.
- cost: The same detail is redrawn, the same mistake is repeated, and a senior architect’s experience goes with them when they leave.
- fix: Project records keep drawings issued, specifications, decisions and site lessons attached to the project. Anyone can open an old project and see what was built, what went wrong on site and what the architect decided. Verity AI can answer questions about it in plain words.
- screen: project

## Day, before and after

Heading: The same Thursday in an architecture practice, before and after.
Lede: The drawing work does not change. What changes is that issues, queries, approvals and stages leave a record, and the record prompts the next step.

- Morning: before: The principal asks three project architects for a status. Each answers from memory, and one says "I think it was sanctioned." | after: The practice screen lists each project’s stage, pending approvals and open site queries. The Monday round takes ten minutes.
- Late morning: before: A contractor calls with a window question. The answer is given on the phone and not noted. | after: The query is logged against the drawing and answered in writing. It is closed in the same sitting.
- Midday: before: A revised drawing is emailed to the structural engineer and the client. The contractor is forgotten. | after: The issue register shows the new revision has not been sent to the contractor, with a task to send it.
- Afternoon: before: A client signs off the schematic stage by message. The accounts person does not hear. | after: The sign-off is recorded and an invoice task lands on accounts that afternoon.
- Evening: before: The senior architect finds out at 6 pm that a submission is due tomorrow and she is also needed on site. | after: The load view showed this clash on Monday. A junior prepared the sheets by Wednesday.
- Month end: before: The practice learns from the accounts that two projects overran on hours. | after: Both were flagged during the work. One had a change charged, one was closed with a repriced next stage.

## Rules and paperwork

Heading: The rules an architecture practice has to keep up with.
Lede: Verity does not give legal or tax advice and does not file anything for you. It keeps the records these rules depend on in order, so you are not rebuilding them from email.
Note: Rules change and differ by state. Confirm current requirements with the Council of Architecture, your lawyer or your accountant. Verity does not file anything.

- **Council of Architecture registration**: what: The Architects Act, 1972 set up the Council of Architecture and provides for registering architects. Only registered individuals may use the title architect, and companies are generally not entitled to practise as architects, so each signing architect in the practice should hold a current certificate. | how: Each architect’s registration number and renewal date are kept on their staff record, and plans or certificates show who signed them. (Council of Architecture: https://coa.gov.in/)
- **Conditions of engagement and scale of charges**: what: The Architects (Professional Conduct) Regulations, 1989 require an architect in private practice to inform the client of the conditions of engagement and scale of charges, which form the basis of the appointment. Fee stages follow from the appointment letter. | how: The appointment letter and fee stages are attached to the project, and stage sign-offs trigger the fee instalment tasks.
- **Building plan sanction and clearances**: what: Sanction is by the local authority under its bye-laws, with extra clearances such as fire or environment depending on the building. Timelines and deemed approval rules differ by state and city. | how: Each approval records the authority, date submitted, queries and replies, and the relevant local timeline you enter. (Sansa Legal: https://www.sansalegal.com/post/how-to-apply-for-a-building-construction-permission-in-india)
- **RERA certificates for developer clients**: what: Under RERA, architects, engineers and chartered accountants certify project information for registered projects, such as the percentage of construction completed. Forms and timing differ by state RERA. | how: Each certificate request is a task with the supporting site visit, the signer and the date, filed on the project.

## Roles

Heading: Everyone sees the same project, through their own window.
Lede: A junior architect should not see what the practice earns on a project. Accounts do not need the drawing set. Each role opens on what it needs.

- Principal: Which projects are healthy and which need me? | Stage and margin per project, approvals waiting, stage fees to bill, team load, overdue invoices.
- Project architect: What is open on my projects today? | Drawing issues, consultant drawings owed, site queries, approvals and client changes.
- Architect or draftsperson: What am I drawing and by when? | Their tasks, the current revision, the brief and their own booked hours.
- Accounts: What can I invoice and what is late? | Signed-off stages awaiting invoice, ageing by client, contractor bills certified.
- Consultant: What drawings do you need from me? | Only the projects they work on, with their drawings owed and open comments.

## FAQ

Heading: Questions principals ask.

- **Will my architects actually use it?** They use only what applies to them: the current drawing issue, their open queries and their weekly hours. We keep it to a few clicks and train on a live project, not in a classroom. They also get a clear list of what is due instead of a stack of emails.
- **Does Verity replace AutoCAD, Revit or my shared drive?** No. Your team keeps drawing in its own tools and files stay where they are. Verity keeps the record around them: issues, revisions, approvals, queries, hours and fees.
- **Can it track municipal approvals for different cities?** Yes. Each approval is a record with its authority and your own timeline for it. Rules and deemed approval periods differ by city and state, so we set these with you and you confirm them locally.
- **We are a six-person practice. Is this too much?** The same leaks exist at six people, with less time to notice them. We set up only what you need, and the four weeks run the same way.
- **Can my structural and MEP consultants be part of it?** Yes, as people with a scope and a list of drawings they owe. They see only the projects they work on, and their drawings and comments appear on the coordination list.
- **Can it help with Council of Architecture fee scales?** It records your own fee stages and instalments for each project and prompts invoicing when a stage is signed off. It does not set your fees or check them against the Council’s scale; that stays your judgement and the appointment letter.
- **Who can see my client data and my fees?** Only the people you allow. Access is set by role, so architects, accounts and consultants each see what they need, and every change is recorded with who made it and when.
- **How long before I see something useful?** The drawing issue register, approval list and stage-to-bill list need only your live projects and fee schedules, so those come first. Margin and load become reliable after a few weeks of logged hours.
- **What does it cost?** It depends on the size of your team and what you want set up. Message us and we will give you a clear number after a short conversation about how your practice runs.
