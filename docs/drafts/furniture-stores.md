## How it runs today

**The showroom looks busy. The back half of the business is running on phone calls.**

- On the floor, a furniture store is easy to read: pieces on display, a customer sitting on a sofa, a salesperson with a quotation pad. What is hard to see is everything that happens after the customer says yes. Which orders are waiting on the factory. Which dining sets are in the godown, wrapped, with no truck booked. Which customer paid an advance five weeks ago and has called three times.
- That knowledge sits in several places at once: a quotation book, a payment register, a delivery diary, a stock sheet that someone updates on Sundays, and the memory of the senior salesperson and the godown supervisor. When one of them is off for a week, the promise made to the customer goes with them.
- Furniture is bought rarely and costs a lot, so every unhappy customer is a lost referral for years. The leak is not a single big mistake. It is a wrong finish, a missed delivery slot, a damaged leg nobody photographed and a balance nobody asked for until the truck was already loaded.
- runs on: Quotation book or Excel; Payment and advance register; Delivery diary and WhatsApp groups; Godown stock sheet; Supplier and carpenter phone calls
- stack note: Each of these does its own job. None of them tells you that order 1184 is promised for Saturday and the sofa is still at the factory.

## Pains

Heading: Where a furniture store actually loses money.
Lede: These are the situations furniture showroom owners describe again and again. For each: what it looks like on an ordinary day, what it costs, and what Verity does about it.

### 1. Walk-ins browse, say they will think about it, and are never called again
- area: Customers
- scene: A couple spends an hour on bedroom sets on Sunday, takes a brochure and leaves. The salesperson jots a number on a slip. By Thursday the slip is under other papers, and the couple has bought from the showroom that phoned them on Monday.
- cost: Example arithmetic: if a showroom sees 120 serious walk-ins a month, loses a fifth of them to nobody following up, and an average sale is ₹55,000, that is about ₹13 lakh of sales a month that simply walked out.
- fix: Every walk-in is recorded as an enquiry against a salesperson with what they looked at, budget and when they plan to buy. Verity lists enquiries with no contact for a set number of days and creates a follow-up task for that salesperson.
- screen: enquiries

### 2. Quotations get lost and nobody knows which version the customer saw
- area: Customers
- scene: The customer was quoted ₹92,000 for a wardrobe on Saturday. On Wednesday the owner quotes ₹88,000 on the phone. Nobody wrote either number down, and a third salesperson now quotes ₹95,000 to the same family.
- cost: Customers who compare notes lose trust, and your best discount gets given twice. A showroom also cannot learn which quotes actually closed.
- fix: Each quotation is a record with the items, price, discount and who approved it, linked to the customer. Revisions keep the old version, and the customer record shows every figure quoted.
- screen: enquiries

### 3. Architects and interior designers send business, and their commission is argued about
- area: Referrals
- scene: A designer brings a client who buys a full living room. A month later the designer calls to ask about the agreed share. The salesperson who made the deal has left, and the arrangement was verbal.
- cost: Underpaid referrers stop sending clients. Overpaid ones eat the margin. In both cases the owner is guessing, because nothing was recorded at the time of sale.
- fix: The referrer is recorded on the enquiry and the order, with the agreed rate. When the order is delivered and paid, Verity lists the commission due to each designer or architect, so settlement is a short list, not a conversation.
- screen: enquiries

### 4. Custom specifications are passed on by word of mouth and built wrong
- area: Orders
- scene: The customer wanted a 7 foot sofa in the teal fabric with the left-hand chaise. The order slip says "sofa, teal". The factory builds the right-hand chaise in a different teal. It arrives after six weeks and has to go back.
- cost: Example arithmetic: one remade ₹70,000 sofa means a second production slot, double freight and a customer who waited three months. Even if the factory pays for the remake, the showroom bears the lost goodwill.
- fix: The order record holds every dimension, fabric code, finish, hand and photo, and the customer signs against that same record. The factory receives the same specification, so the thing built matches the thing sold.
- screen: order

### 5. Advance taken, delivery date promised, then the date slips and nobody tells the customer
- area: Orders
- scene: A customer paid ₹30,000 advance on a dining set and was told "four weeks". It is week six. She calls the showroom every few days and each time a different person says "I will check and call you back".
- cost: Long waits on paid orders are among the most common furniture complaints in Indian consumer forums. Beyond the lost customer, the showroom holds a customer's money with no clear commitment behind it.
- fix: Each order carries a promised date and a current stage. When the date is at risk, Verity raises it to the order owner before the customer calls, so the revised date is given by you, with a reason, and recorded on the order.
- screen: making
- source: (Examples of delayed-delivery complaints, consumercomplaints.in: https://www.consumercomplaints.in/jodhpuri-furniture-delayed-and-missing-furniture-c3226707)

### 6. You cannot see where the order is inside the factory or workshop
- area: Suppliers
- scene: Half your range is made by two or three factories and a few carpenters. The only way to learn whether the almirah is cut, polished or packed is to call. The factory manager says "this week", every week.
- cost: A showroom that cannot see production cannot promise dates honestly. Staff spend hours a week on status calls that add nothing to the business.
- fix: Each purchase order to a factory or workshop has stages (cutting, finishing, upholstery, packed, dispatched) with a date for each. The supplier or your coordinator updates the stage, and the customer order shows it.
- screen: making

### 7. Display pieces and slow movers sit for months and eat showroom rent
- area: Stock
- scene: A glass-top dining table has stood in the corner since last Diwali. Three sofa models that looked good in the catalogue have not sold. Every square foot of display is paid for in rent.
- cost: Retail trade commentary lists rent, staff and inventory costs and slow-moving stock as the main strain on furniture stores in 2026. A piece that has stood for eight months has already cost its margin in floor space.
- fix: Every piece has an age in the showroom and godown. Verity lists items past your cut-off, with the money tied up, and suggests the options you decide on: markdown, ex-display sale, swap to another outlet or return to supplier.
- screen: stock
- source: (InteriorDaily, 22 May 2026, quoting the CEO of Priti International: https://www.interiordaily.com/article/9840066/the-indian-furniture-sector-has-long-term-potential-but-the-industry-is-facing-several-real-challenges/)

### 8. The piece you sold is not in the godown, or is sold twice
- area: Stock
- scene: Two salespeople promise the last oak bed in the display finish to two customers on the same afternoon. Or the stock sheet says four coffee tables and the godown has two, because the sheet is updated on Sundays.
- cost: One customer is told the item is gone after agreeing to buy it. Showrooms also reorder too late or too early because the count in the sheet is a guess.
- fix: Stock is held per location (showroom, godown, factory in progress). A sale reserves the exact piece, so the second salesperson sees it is taken, and every movement between locations is recorded against a transfer.
- screen: stock

### 9. Delivery slots clash with lifts, floors, building rules and truck availability
- area: Delivery
- scene: The sofa is loaded at 9 am. At the building the lift is booked until noon, the society allows deliveries only until 2 pm, and the crew cannot carry a 3-seater up four floors. The customer takes a half-day off for nothing.
- cost: A failed delivery costs a truck trip, a crew day and a customer's trust. Heavy furniture delivery in India often takes one to three weeks, and a missed slot adds more.
- fix: Delivery details (floor, lift size, society timings, contact person, access photos) are captured on the order. Verity builds the day's runs by truck and crew, and flags orders where the balance is unpaid or an item is not yet ready.
- screen: delivery
- source: (Industry commentary on heavy furniture lead times, Startupfeed: https://startupfeed.in/why-indias-furniture-ecommerce-is-growing-up-wakefits-ipo-pepperfrys-fall-and-what-comes-next/)

### 10. Damage in transit is found at the door and cannot be claimed
- area: Delivery
- scene: The leg of the dining table is cracked. The packing was fine when it left the godown, or so the supervisor says. There is no photo, the transporter says the receipt was signed clean, and the factory says it left perfect.
- cost: Without proof, the showroom absorbs the loss. Furniture returns and damage costs are high because logistics firms often lack experience handling bulky pieces.
- fix: The delivery record has a check at dispatch and at handover with photos attached, and the customer's remarks. A damage becomes a claim record against the transporter or supplier with the evidence in one place and a deadline.
- screen: delivery

### 11. Assembly and installation visits are forgotten or incomplete
- area: Delivery
- scene: The wardrobe was delivered in parts on Friday. The carpenter was supposed to fit it on Saturday but was sent to another job. The customer has panels leaning on the wall and a house-warming on Sunday.
- cost: Many of the worst reviews are about the days after delivery, not the product. A missing set of screws also means a second visit, which is a cost with no revenue behind it.
- fix: Assembly is a task on the same order with a date, a carpenter and a short checklist (all parts present, fitted, cleaned, customer signed off). Verity shows what is delivered but not yet fitted.
- screen: delivery

### 12. Balances are chased after delivery instead of before it
- area: Money
- scene: The truck is loaded for a ₹1.4 lakh order with ₹60,000 outstanding. The driver is told "the customer will pay on delivery". The customer is not home, the cheque is "in the mail", and the furniture is now in the flat.
- cost: Example arithmetic: ten orders a month with ₹40,000 outstanding at delivery is ₹4 lakh of cash waiting on goodwill, plus the time of whoever calls for it.
- fix: The order shows advance, balance and due date. The delivery plan flags unpaid balances before the truck is loaded, and payments (cash, UPI, cheque, finance) are recorded against the order as they arrive.
- screen: order

### 13. Warranty and repair complaints arrive on WhatsApp and are lost
- area: After-sales
- scene: A customer messages that the sofa fabric is pilling after a year. Nobody knows the purchase date, the fabric code or the warranty terms. The message sits in a personal phone and is answered two weeks later.
- cost: Furniture is bought rarely, so the customer you have is your referral for the next purchase. Unanswered complaints also appear as public reviews.
- fix: Each complaint is a case on the customer record with the original order, warranty term, photos and an owner. Verity tracks the case to a visit, repair, replacement or closure, and shows how many are open and how old.
- screen: aftersales

### 14. Discounts and incentives are decided at the counter and calculated by hand
- area: People
- scene: Salespeople offer "a bit off" to close a sale. At month end the owner adds up incentives from the order book, one salesperson says a sale was missed, and the strongest seller starts looking at a competitor.
- cost: Uncontrolled discounting is a direct margin leak, and a salesperson who feels short-changed takes their customers when they go.
- fix: Discounts above a limit wait for an approval with a reason. Every sale is recorded against the salesperson, so the incentive statement is built from the records and each person can see their own.
- screen: owner

### 15. Several showrooms and a godown, and no single view of what is selling
- area: Owner
- scene: The main showroom sells out of a recliner while a second branch has three unsold. Weekly numbers arrive on WhatsApp in different formats. Festive season stocking is decided by gut feel.
- cost: Stock sits idle in one place while another loses sales. The owner finds out about a weak range after a season, not during it.
- fix: Each showroom and godown is a location in one system. Sales, stock age, pending orders and collections can be compared side by side, and a transfer between locations is a recorded movement.
- screen: owner

## Day, before and after

Heading: The same Saturday, before and after.
Lede: The work does not change. The difference is that every promise leaves a record, and the record does the chasing.

## Rules and paperwork

Heading: The rules a furniture store has to keep up with.
Lede: Verity does not give legal or tax advice and does not file anything for you. It keeps the records these rules lean on in order, so your accountant is not rebuilding them from a quotation book.
Note: Rules change and differ by state and by product. Confirm current requirements with your accountant, the BIS or the relevant authority.

- **GST on furniture and parts**: what: Furniture under HSN 9403 generally carries 18% after the September 2025 revisions, but rates differ by item and can change. Mattresses and cushions sit under a different heading. | how: Each order line carries its tax category, so the split your accountant needs is clean, and advances are recorded separately from final invoices. (Tally Solutions guide to HSN 9403: https://tallysolutions.com/gst/hsn-code-9403-product-classification-gst-rate-business-filing-guide/)
- **BIS standards for covered furniture**: what: Under the Furniture (Quality Control) Order, 2025, work chairs, general purpose chairs and stools, tables and desks, storage units, beds and bunk beds must meet the matching Indian Standard and carry the BIS Standard Mark. Start dates and exemptions for small makers were amended, so confirm the current position. | how: The licence or mark details of covered products are kept on the product record, so you can see which suppliers have supplied them before a piece goes on the floor. (Furniture (Quality Control) Order, 2025, via Legitquest: https://www.legitquest.com/act/furniture-quality-control-order-2025/10996)
- **Consumer protection and written promises**: what: Under India's consumer protection law, a customer can complain about defective goods or deficient service, including delay on a paid order. Your written order, promised date and warranty terms are your evidence. | how: Specification, promised dates, revised dates with reasons and signed handover are on the order record with who changed what and when.
- **Staff records under state shop rules**: what: Most states require records of working hours, weekly offs and leave for showroom staff under their Shops and Establishments rules. Details differ by state. | how: Shifts, attendance and leave are recorded against each person, including delivery crews and carpenters on your payroll.

## FAQ

Heading: Questions furniture store owners ask.

- **Will my salespeople actually enter orders properly?** It only works if the order is recorded, so the order form is built from the slip they already write and takes a few minutes. Salespeople get something back: their own enquiries, order status and incentive statement, which saves them from being asked where things are.
- **We make most pieces to order through workshops. Can Verity track that?** Yes. Each customer order links to a purchase order with a workshop or factory, and the stages you use (cutting, polish, upholstery, packed) are set up in week 2. The workshop or your coordinator updates the stage and the customer order shows it.
- **Does Verity handle my billing?** No. Your billing software keeps issuing invoices. We agree during setup how orders and payments reach Verity, which holds the order, the promise and the money still due.
- **Can it message my customers about delivery dates?** Not by itself. Verity flags that a date has moved and creates the task, your team calls or sends the message, and the outcome is recorded on the order.
- **I have two showrooms and a godown. Is that a problem?** No, it is the normal case. Each is a location in one system, stock is held per location, and a transfer between them is a recorded movement. Sales, order backlogs and stock age can be compared side by side.
- **Who can see my margins and cost prices?** Only the people you allow. Access is set by role, so a salesperson can see selling prices and their own results while cost prices stay with you and your accountant. Every change is recorded with who made it and when.
- **Can it handle designer and architect commissions?** Yes. The referrer and agreed rate are recorded on the enquiry and the order. When the order is delivered and paid, the commission shows on a payable list, and you mark it settled.
- **Can it track damage and warranty claims against suppliers?** Yes. Damage found at delivery becomes a claim record with the photos, the order and the supplier or transporter. Warranty complaints are cases linked to the original order and its warranty term.
- **What does it cost?** It depends on the number of showrooms, staff and what you want set up. Message us and we will give you a clear number after a short conversation about how your orders move.
