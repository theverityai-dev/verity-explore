## How it runs today

**The agency's most valuable asset is in people's phones.**

- A PR agency of ten to fifty people serves a dozen or more clients, each with announcements, spokespeople and a monthly report to show. Media relationships are held by individuals. A senior consultant knows which reporter covers funding, who prefers a call and who was burned by a late embargo. When she leaves, the knowledge leaves.
- The working records are in pieces. Media lists are Excel sheets that differ by person. Pitches go out from personal email and WhatsApp. Coverage is saved as screenshots and links. The retainer is a monthly fee that has not changed since the day it was signed, while the work has grown.
- So the agency learns late. Two team members pitch the same reporter on the same day. A client is told coverage is "coming" with no record of who was pitched. A monthly report is built in a night from folders. And a retainer sold for 20 hours takes 45. A trade survey of Indian PR professionals in 2025 found two in three say securing editorial coverage has become harder, and 66% find outdated client expectations a major challenge.
- runs on: Excel media lists per team; Personal email and WhatsApp for pitches; Shared folders of clippings; A media monitoring service; Timesheets or none
- stack note: Each one does its job. Nothing joins the journalist, the pitch, the coverage and the retainer.

## Pains

Heading: Where a PR agency actually loses money.
Lede: These are the situations PR founders and account heads describe again and again. For each one: what it looks like, what it costs, and what Verity does about it.

### 1. Media lists differ by person
- area: Media lists
- scene: Four consultants each keep their own list of tech reporters. One has a new number for a reporter, another still uses the old one. Nobody knows who left which publication.
- cost: Pitches go to the wrong person or a dead email, and a good story gets no coverage because the contact was out of date.
- fix: Journalists are records with beat, outlet, contact details, preferred way to be reached, and last verified date. Everyone works from the same list, and a record not checked for six months is flagged.
- screen: medialist

### 2. The same reporter is pitched twice in a day
- area: Media lists
- scene: A business reporter gets a funding story from one consultant at 10 am and an unrelated product pitch from another at 4 pm. She replies to neither and marks both as noise.
- cost: A relationship built over years is spent on careless contact. Reporters talk to each other about agencies that spam.
- fix: Every pitch is logged on the journalist's record with the client and the result. Before a pitch is sent, the consultant sees contact in the last 14 days and who made it.
- screen: medialist

### 3. Pitch follow-ups depend on memory
- area: Pitches
- scene: A pitch went out on Tuesday. The plan was to call on Thursday. Thursday brings a client crisis and the follow-up is never made.
- cost: A story that needed one nudge lapses. The client asks why nothing has run and the honest answer is that nobody called.
- fix: Each pitch has a follow-up date and an owner. Verity lists pitches awaiting a response, due today, and marks the outcome: covered, declined, no response.
- screen: pipeline

### 4. Embargoes and announcement dates collide
- area: Pitches
- scene: Two clients plan announcements for the same Tuesday. A third client's embargoed news has to break at 6 am. Briefings are being given by the same person.
- cost: A clash dilutes both stories. A broken embargo damages trust with reporters and the client.
- fix: Announcements are on a shared calendar with date, time, embargo state, spokesperson and the consultant responsible. Clashes show as the plan is made, and a story with an embargo is marked until release.
- screen: pipeline

### 5. Spokespeople and approvals
- area: Approvals
- scene: A quote for a founder goes to the client by chat. The founder edits it twice by voice note. The version sent to the reporter is the first one.
- cost: A misquote or an unapproved line printed under the client's name is hard to take back.
- fix: Statements and releases have versions and a named client approver. Only the approved version can be marked as sent, and the approval date stays on the record.
- screen: pipeline

### 6. Coverage is collected by hand on the 30th
- area: Coverage
- scene: On the last day of the month, an executive searches each outlet for the client's name, takes screenshots and pastes links into a sheet. Some pieces are missed. Others are old.
- cost: Example arithmetic: 15 clients at 5 hours each is 75 hours a month, nearly two weeks of one person, on a task that proves value but builds none.
- fix: Coverage is logged as it appears, against the client, the pitch it came from and the journalist. The monthly view builds from those records, so the report starts on day 1 and not day 30.
- screen: coverage

### 7. Nobody can show what the coverage was worth
- area: Coverage
- scene: A client's CMO asks, "We got 40 mentions. So what?" The agency shows a pile of links. Only 46% of corporate communication respondents in a PRCAI study said they have a separate measurement budget, and 92% said the industry needs a clear, standard measurement system.
- cost: Without agreed measures, the client judges PR by vibes and the retainer is the first cost cut in a bad quarter.
- fix: Each client's goals are set on the record at the start: key messages, target outlets and spokespeople. Every piece of coverage is tagged against them, so the monthly view shows what moved against what was agreed.
- screen: coverage
- source: (exchange4media on PRCAI SPRINT 2026, 2026: https://www.exchange4media.com/pr-and-corporate-communication-news/indian-public-relations-market-to-reach-rs4500-crore-by-2030-prcai-sprint-2026-156024.html)

### 8. The retainer that never rises while scope does
- area: Retainers
- scene: A retainer was agreed at ₹80,000 a month for media relations. It now includes social posts, event support, a founder's LinkedIn ghostwriting and an investor note.
- cost: One PR founder's published opinion piece puts it plainly: the scope keeps growing while the retainer stays the same. Example arithmetic: 55 hours against 30 sold, at ₹900 an hour of cost, is ₹22,500 a month absorbed.
- fix: Each retainer lists its inclusions and sold hours. Time is logged against the client as work is done, and Verity shows hours used against hours sold and any extra work waiting for an approval before it begins.
- screen: retainer
- source: (PRmoment India, 2024: https://www.prmoment.in/opinion/pr-retainers-the-flat-cake-that-never-rises)

### 9. Clients with outdated expectations
- area: Retainers
- scene: A client expects a front-page story every month because that is what PR meant ten years ago. The agency is delivering relevant coverage in the right outlets but cannot show why that is better.
- cost: In a 2025 India survey, 66% of PR professionals found managing outdated client expectations a major challenge. The mismatch ends in a lost client.
- fix: The goal and agreed outlets are on the client record from the first week. Reviews are scheduled and recorded, with notes on what was agreed, so expectations are reset against a written record.
- screen: retainer
- source: (PRmoment India, PR Agency Insights Survey 2025: https://prmoment.in/pr-insight/is-earned-media-broken-latest-pr-agency-research-shows-challenges-and-potential-fixes)

### 10. A crisis starts on a Saturday night
- area: Crisis
- scene: A negative post about a client goes viral at 10 pm. The only person with the client's crisis contacts is on a flight. The statement draft is in her laptop.
- cost: The first few hours decide the story. Delay and confusion make it larger.
- fix: Each client has a crisis record with named contacts, approvers, a holding statement and an escalation order. When an issue is raised, Verity creates tasks for each role and keeps one log of what was said and approved.
- screen: crisis

### 11. Influencer and sponsored content without the right label
- area: Compliance
- scene: A creator campaign for a client goes out with the disclosure buried in a hashtag block.
- cost: ASCI's scorecard found 69 of the top 100 influencers failed its disclosure guidelines, so the risk is common. A complaint names the brand and the agency that set it up.
- fix: Creator and sponsored posts have a checklist for disclosure and claims, a reviewer and the live link recorded. The task closes only after the check.
- screen: crisis
- source: (exchange4media on ASCI study, 2025: https://www.exchange4media.com/advertising-news/69-of-indias-top-100-influencers-dont-comply-with-guidelines-asci-study-140688.html)

### 12. Journalists' personal details and client data
- area: Compliance
- scene: A media list with personal phone numbers is forwarded by email to a freelancer. A client's investor list is saved on three laptops.
- cost: The DPDP Rules, notified in November 2025, set duties on personal data, with most starting about 18 months after publication. Loose copies of lists become a risk for you and your clients.
- fix: Lists are records with permissions and an audit trail. Retention dates are set, and a task appears to remove access for a freelancer when a project closes.
- screen: medialist
- source: (Scconline on DPDP Rules 2025: https://www.scconline.com/blog/post/2025/12/26/digital-personal-data-protection-rules-2025-key-highlights/)

### 13. Pitching for new business while serving old
- area: Growth
- scene: The founder is pitching a new client and also handling a crisis for an old one. The proposal is late, and the team's hours for the pitch are not counted anywhere.
- cost: The agency's new business depends on the same few seniors who also hold the key relationships. Overload loses both.
- fix: New business is a pipeline with stage, value, owner and next step. Load by person is visible next to live work, so the pitch is staffed knowingly.
- screen: owner

- we: Set up client records with goals and key messages, journalist records with beats, pitch stages, embargo states, approval steps, retainer inclusions and the six screens in your own terms.

- we: Stay on after launch. Review the first month's coverage logs and retainer hours with you, tune the stages and limits, and handle changes when you add a client or a team.

## Rules and paperwork

Heading: The rules a PR agency has to keep up with.
Lede: Verity does not give legal or tax advice and does not file anything for you. It keeps the records these rules depend on in order, so you are not hunting for them when someone asks.
Note: Rules change and differ by situation. Confirm current requirements with your accountant or lawyer.

- **ASCI and CCPA rules on sponsored and influencer content**: what: ASCI's influencer guidelines and the CCPA guidelines generally require prominent disclosure of any material connection, such as a paid or gifted post. ASCI found 69 of the top 100 influencers failed in its 2025 scorecard. | how: Creator and sponsored posts have a checklist with a reviewer and the live link recorded. (exchange4media on ASCI study: https://www.exchange4media.com/advertising-news/69-of-indias-top-100-influencers-dont-comply-with-guidelines-asci-study-140688.html)

Most duties, including notice, security safeguards, breach reporting and retention, generally apply 18 months after publication. Media lists and investor lists hold personal data. | how: Lists are records with permissions and an audit trail, with retention dates and removal tasks. (Scconline on DPDP Rules: https://www.scconline.com/blog/post/2025/12/26/digital-personal-data-protection-rules-2025-key-highlights/)
- **Client contract, confidentiality and embargo terms**: what: Your contract generally decides what the retainer includes, confidentiality and how embargoed news is handled. Verity does not draft contracts. | how: Embargo dates and terms are on each announcement record, and the contract date and inclusions are on the retainer.
