## How it runs today

**A dental clinic is a chain of small steps that nobody sees end to end.**

- The first visit goes well. The plan is explained, the patient agrees, a root canal is started, an impression is taken, and a crown is ordered from the lab. Then comes the waiting: for the patient to return, for the lab to deliver, for the next slot to open. Each wait is a place where the plan can stall.
- The facts sit in different places. The chart is on paper or in a basic software screen. Fees paid and fees due are in the accounts register. Lab cases are in a notebook and on WhatsApp with the technician. Consumables are counted when the assistant says something has run out. The clinic owner, who is usually also the main dentist, holds the whole picture in their head between patients.
- Nothing here is a crisis. The cost is slower: patients who half finish treatment, crowns that arrive after the patient has stopped caring, chairs that sit idle after a late cancellation, and material that expires in the cupboard.
- runs on: Paper charts or basic dental software; Fee register or accounts sheet; Lab cases in a notebook; WhatsApp with the lab and patients; Consumables counted by eye
- stack note: Each one does its own small job. None of them can tell you which patient needs a call today.

## Pains

Heading: Where a dental clinic loses money and patients.
Lede: These are the situations dentists and clinic managers describe. For each one: what it looks like, what it costs, and what Verity does about it. Verity records what your team decides; it never decides treatment.

### 1. Accepted plans stall after the first visits
- area: Treatment plans
- scene: A patient agrees to a root canal, a crown and a scaling. The canal is opened, a temporary filling is placed, and the patient says they will call for the next slot. Three weeks later the temporary is still there and nobody has called.
- cost: Example arithmetic: ten stalled plans a month with ₹12,000 of work still pending each is ₹1.2 lakh of accepted treatment that never completes. There is also a clinical risk to the patient when a temporary stays too long.
- fix: Each plan is a list of steps with a state and a patient. Verity shows plans with a step started but not finished, how long they have waited, and assigns a follow-up task to the front desk. The outcome of each call is recorded.
- screen: plans

### 2. Recall visits for scaling and check-ups are never made
- area: Treatment plans
- scene: Patients are told to come back in six months. The date is written on a card or not at all. Most come only when something hurts, and the regulars who did come last year are not reminded.
- cost: A recall base is the steadiest income a clinic has. Without a list, it depends on patients remembering and on the assistant noticing a card.
- fix: The recall interval is recorded with the visit. Verity lists patients due this month, grouped by type of recall, and creates a call task per patient. Staff make the call; the result is recorded as booked, declined or unreachable.
- screen: plans

### 3. Empty chairs after last-minute cancellations
- area: Chairs and time
- scene: The 11 am patient cancels at 10:40. The 4 pm patient does not turn up. The same few patients do it again and again, but each time it looks like a one-off.
- cost: A chair, an assistant and the dentist all stand idle. Studies of missed dental appointments put the rate anywhere from under one in ten to well over a third, depending on the clinic and how it is measured.
- fix: Cancellations and no-shows are recorded against the patient, so repeat cases are visible. Verity lists the cancelled slots with the patients waiting for an earlier date, so the desk can fill them. A rule can flag anyone with two earlier no-shows for a confirmation call.
- screen: owner
- source: (Missed appointments in a pediatric dental clinic, Navi Mumbai (PMC, 2018): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6102432)

### 4. Doctors and chairs are unevenly used, and nobody has the numbers
- area: Chairs and time
- scene: The orthodontist is booked a fortnight ahead on Saturdays. The visiting endodontist has two patients on Tuesday. Chair 3 is used half the day while chair 1 has a queue.
- cost: A staffed hour that goes unused is gone. Without numbers, the clinic cannot tell whether to add a visiting day, move a shift, or add a chair.
- fix: Procedures are recorded against the doctor, chair and time. Utilisation by chair, doctor and day is a live view, with the average length of each procedure type compared to the slot booked for it.
- screen: owner

### 5. Crowns and dentures come back late, wrong or not at all
- area: Lab work
- scene: A crown was due on Thursday. On Thursday the patient arrives and the box is not there. The technician says it will go out tomorrow. Last month a shade came back a tone off and the case had to be redone.
- cost: A wasted chair slot, an annoyed patient, and a remake that the clinic usually pays for. Late cases also push every following visit back.
- fix: Every lab order is a record: patient, tooth, shade, lab, date sent, date due, date received. Verity shows what is overdue, flags cases due within two days with no dispatch, and keeps a count of remakes per lab.
- screen: lab

### 6. Which lab is actually good is a matter of opinion
- area: Lab work
- scene: Three labs send work to the clinic. One is cheaper but slow, one is fast but has more remakes. The owner has a feeling about each but no record.
- cost: You may be sending your most demanding cases to the wrong lab, or paying a premium for a speed you do not actually receive.
- fix: Because each case records the lab, the due date, the date received and whether it was remade, Verity gives each lab a simple scorecard of average turnaround, late cases and remakes. Pricing sits beside it.
- screen: lab

### 7. Consumables run out in the middle of a procedure
- area: Materials
- scene: The assistant opens the cupboard for the composite shade the patient needs, and it is empty. The anaesthetic cartridge box has one left. A rushed order goes out by phone.
- cost: A procedure postponed or done with a second-best material. Rush orders often cost more than a planned one.
- fix: Each material has a reorder level and a usual supplier. Usage is recorded with procedures, so Verity warns before the cupboard is empty and drafts an order to the supplier for you to approve.
- screen: stock

### 8. Materials expire unused, and prices creep up unnoticed
- area: Materials
- scene: A box of impression material bought on a scheme sits at the back for a year. A supplier raises the price of bonding agent twice and the invoice is simply paid.
- cost: Stock that expires is paid for and thrown away. Small price rises, unwatched, add to every procedure’s cost.
- fix: Batches and expiry dates are recorded at receipt. Verity lists what expires in 90 days, and shows the last prices paid per item and supplier, so a creeping rise is visible when the invoice is entered.
- screen: stock

### 9. Sterilisation, waste and X-ray records live in separate notebooks
- area: Compliance
- scene: The autoclave cycle log, the bio-medical waste register and the X-ray exposure log are three different books, filled in at different times by different people. When an inspector or an auditor asks, someone goes looking.
- cost: A gap in these records is a compliance finding, and in the worst case a safety question that you cannot answer from paper.
- fix: Each register is a daily checklist assigned to a named person. Verity shows what has been completed and what is missing, and keeps the history searchable and printable.
- screen: compliance

### 10. Consent, images and notes are scattered, and a patient asks for a copy
- area: Records
- scene: Written consent for an extraction is in a paper folder. The OPG is on the X-ray machine’s computer. The treatment notes are in the chart. A patient’s lawyer asks for a complete copy.
- cost: Finding and assembling the record takes staff time, and an incomplete set can be a problem in a dispute. Dentists are generally expected to keep records for a minimum period and produce them on a valid request.
- fix: The consent, notes, images and payment history sit on one patient record with permissions. A complete set can be assembled from one place, and every view and change is logged.
- screen: patient
- source: (Dentists Code of Ethics Regulations, 2014 (state dental council copy): https://tndentalcouncil.com/source/dentists_code_of_ethics_regulations_2014.pdf)

### 11. Part payments on long treatments get lost
- area: Money
- scene: A ₹36,000 implant plan is paid in three parts. The patient paid the first, the second was paid in cash on a day the receipt book was with the owner, and the third is not recorded. The fee register says one thing, the chair side says another.
- cost: Unrecorded payments become disputes. Unrecorded dues become write-offs, and patients with a balance can be hard to chase months later.
- fix: Each plan has an agreed amount and every payment against it. Verity shows balance by patient, flags plans where treatment has gone ahead of payment, and lists dues ageing past 30 days.
- screen: dues

### 12. Insurance and corporate tie-up claims wait in a file
- area: Money
- scene: A company’s employee dental scheme and a handful of insured patients are billed separately. Claim forms are sent, the clinic waits, and nobody has a list of what is pending with whom.
- cost: Money that is owed but not chased. Claims not filed in time may not be paid at all.
- fix: A claim is a record with the patient, insurer or company, amount, date sent and state. Verity lists those unpaid past their usual period and tasks the person who sends them.
- screen: dues

### 13. Visiting specialists and associates are paid by hand-worked shares
- area: People
- scene: An orthodontist takes 40 percent of fees on their patients, an associate dentist takes a slab, and an assistant has a monthly bonus tied to procedures. Month end means someone with a calculator and a stack of receipts.
- cost: Hours of owner time, errors that cost either side, and arguments that sour a relationship you want to keep.
- fix: Because procedures and payments are recorded against the treating doctor, Verity builds a payout statement from your own agreed shares. The doctor sees the same breakdown. Your accountant handles the actual payment.
- screen: dues

### 14. The equipment fails when it is needed, and the second clinic runs blind
- area: Owner
- scene: The compressor stops during a morning list. The autoclave’s annual service was due in July. The second branch is run from a WhatsApp group and a nightly figure from the manager.
- cost: A morning of cancelled patients, or an expensive emergency call. For two branches, no fair comparison and a slow warning when something is off.
- fix: Equipment is a record with a service schedule, a vendor and a history. Verity tasks the service before it is due. Branches are locations in one system, with a patient seen at both shown as one person.
- screen: owner

## How we work

Heading: We set it up with you, over four weeks.
Lede: You will not get a login and a manual. We learn how your clinic runs, build Verity around it, bring your records in, then stay to make sure it keeps working.

### Week 1: Discovery and mapping
- we: Spend a day in the clinic. Follow a patient from the front desk through the chair to the payment, see how a lab case travels, and look at how plans, fees and materials are recorded today.
- you: A few hours of yours and your front desk lead’s. Access to the chart system or paper folders, the fee register and the lab notebook.
- get: A written map of your clinic: doctors, chairs, treatments and price list, labs and suppliers, plus the problems we will fix first.

### Weeks 2 to 3: Configuration
- we: Set up your treatments, plan stages, recall intervals, lab list, materials with reorder levels, doctor shares, daily registers and branches, then build the screens in your clinic’s words.
- you: Check the setup with your senior assistant. Correct any rule we got wrong, such as how you split a visiting doctor’s fee.

## FAQ

Heading: Questions dentists ask.

- **Does Verity make clinical decisions or suggest treatment?** No. Your dentists decide diagnosis and treatment. Verity records the plan your dentist writes, tracks the steps and tells your team what is waiting.
- **We already use dental charting software. Do we replace it?** Not necessarily. If you chart and store images in a dedicated tool, you can keep it. During setup we agree how the information Verity needs reaches it, such as plan steps and procedures done.
- **Can Verity send reminders to patients?** Not by itself. It builds the recall and follow-up lists and assigns the call or message to a named person on your team. They send it, and the result is recorded.
- **How does it deal with part payments on a long treatment?** Each plan has an agreed amount, and each payment is recorded against it. You see the balance by patient and which plans have moved ahead of payment.
- **Is it too much for a one-chair clinic?** A one-chair clinic has the same stalled plans and late crowns, with less time to notice them. We set up only what you need. The four-week process is the same.
- **Can I run two or three branches in one view?** Yes. Each branch is a location in one system. A patient who visits both is one person, and chair use, dues and stock compare side by side.
- **Who can see patient data?** Only the people you allow. Access is by role, so the front desk, doctors, assistants and your accountant each see only what they need. Every view and change is logged with the person and time.
- **How do we handle consent forms and images?** Consent and notes are attached to the patient record. For images, we agree during setup whether they stay in your imaging software with a link on the record, or are stored with the patient.
- **What does it cost?** It depends on the number of clinics, doctors and what you want set up. Message us, and after a short conversation about how your clinic runs we will give you a clear number.
