## How it runs today

**Most practices are a few clinicians, a shared calendar and many private chats.**

- A solo counsellor keeps appointments in a phone calendar and notes in a notebook or a locked file. A group practice adds a front desk, a shared sheet and a WhatsApp group where clients, therapists and the coordinator all talk about scheduling.
- The hard part is that this information is among the most sensitive a business can hold. India's Mental Healthcare Act 2017 gives a person with mental illness a right to confidentiality, a duty on health professionals to keep information confidential, and confidentiality that applies to information stored in electronic or digital form. A shared sheet and open chat are poor places for it.
- The need is large and supply is small. The 2015-16 National Mental Health Survey found treatment gaps of about 85 percent for common disorders and 73.6 percent for severe ones, and a 2023 parliamentary committee noted about 0.75 psychiatrists per lakh people. A clinician's time is scarce, and losing it to admin is expensive.
- runs on: Phone or Google calendar; Notebooks or locked files for notes; Excel for clients and fees; WhatsApp with clients and between clinicians; UPI and cash receipts
- stack note: Each tool is private enough on its own. Together they have no consistent rule on who may see what, and no one is watching for the client who has quietly stopped coming.

## Pains

Heading: Where a mental wellness practice loses time, clients and peace of mind.
Lede: These are the situations therapists, counsellors and practice coordinators describe. For each one: what it looks like on an ordinary day, what it costs, and what Verity does about it.

### 1. Late cancellations and empty hours
- area: Sessions
- scene: A client cancels forty minutes before a session. Another does not turn up. The hour cannot be refilled, and nobody can say whether it is a pattern for this client or only today.
- cost: Example arithmetic: a therapist with 24 sessions a week at ₹1,500 who loses three a week to late cancellations loses ₹4,500 a week, about ₹18,000 a month.
- fix: Each session is a record with a status: booked, confirmed, attended, cancelled late, no-show. Verity shows late cancellations by client and clinician, flags the pattern and keeps your cancellation terms on the record. Staff speak to clients; Verity records the outcome.
- screen: sessions

### 2. Schedules across clinicians and rooms
- area: Sessions
- scene: Two clinicians share three rooms. A couple's session needs a bigger room. A clinician moves a day and the front desk finds out from the client. Online and in-person sessions are mixed on the same calendar.
- cost: Double-booked rooms and last-minute changes damage the sense of safety a client expects.
- fix: Clinicians, rooms and online slots sit on one schedule. A change shows to everyone who needs it, and the desk can see free rooms by hour.
- screen: sessions

### 3. The client who quietly stops coming
- area: Clients
- scene: A client attended weekly for two months, then missed two sessions and did not reply. Nobody on the team noticed, because each clinician sees only their own diary.
- cost: Studies of Indian psychiatric outpatients report dropout rates of 21 to 59 percent in some settings. A lost client is lost income and, more importantly, a break in someone's care.
- fix: Each client's session pattern is recorded. When a client misses beyond a gap the clinician sets, Verity lists them for a gentle check-in task for the clinician or coordinator. Staff reach out; Verity records the result.
- screen: clients

### 4. Follow-ups, homework and review dates are in a notebook
- area: Clients
- scene: The therapist agrees to review in six weeks and to send a worksheet. The note is in a book. When the date arrives nobody remembers.
- cost: A forgotten commitment hurts the therapeutic relationship and the practice's reputation.
- fix: A follow-up is a task with a date and an owner linked to the client. Review dates and shared documents show on the clinician's list for the day.
- screen: clients

### 5. Intake forms and consent sit in different places
- area: Consent
- scene: A new client fills a form on paper, signs a consent for online sessions on WhatsApp, and a guardian's consent for a minor is a photo on someone's phone. The practice cannot quickly show what each client agreed to.
- cost: Without a clear record of consent, a practice cannot show what was agreed, and cannot respect a client's changes of mind.
- fix: Intake details, consent for treatment, for online sessions, for sharing with a family member or referrer, and a guardian's consent are records with a date and the version agreed. A withdrawn consent is recorded and visible to anyone who needs to act on it.
- screen: access

### 6. Session notes are kept in unsafe places
- area: Confidentiality
- scene: Notes are in a shared drive folder the front desk can open. A therapist types notes into a WhatsApp chat to herself. A laptop is shared at home. A relative of a client calls the desk and asks if their brother attends.
- cost: Breach of confidentiality can end a therapeutic relationship, expose the practice to complaints and, under the Mental Healthcare Act 2017, breach a duty set in law. Confidentiality under that Act is stated to apply to digital information also.
- fix: Notes are restricted documents. Only the clinician who wrote them, and anyone that clinician explicitly allows, can open them. The desk sees appointments and fees, not content. Every open of a record is logged.
- screen: access
- source: (Mental Healthcare Act 2017 text, sections 23 to 25: https://www.legalauthority.in/bare-act/mental-healthcare-act-2017)

### 7. Who is asking: relatives, employers and insurers
- area: Confidentiality
- scene: A parent phones to ask if their adult daughter attended. An employer asks for a fitness letter. An insurer asks for records. The front desk is not sure what it may say.
- cost: An improvised answer is the commonest way confidentiality is broken.
- fix: Each request is recorded as a task for the clinician with the requester, the date and the client's consent status shown. The desk's script is the same every time: the clinician will respond. The reply and its basis are recorded.
- screen: access

### 8. A client asks to see their records
- area: Records
- scene: A client asks for a copy of their file. Notes are in several places. The clinician is unsure what may be shared and what may be withheld.
- cost: Section 25 of the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 gives a person with mental illness a right to access basic medical records, and a professional may withhold information in some cases, and must then tell the person they can apply to the Board. Handling this ad hoc is risky.
- fix: A records request is a task with a due date and the clinician's decision recorded, including any withheld portion and the notice given. Verity organises the request. The clinician decides what to share.
- screen: access
- source: (Mental Healthcare Act 2017 text: https://www.legalauthority.in/bare-act/mental-healthcare-act-2017)

### 9. Teleconsultation and online session records
- area: Sessions
- scene: Online sessions are held on a call app. Consent for online sessions, the platform used, and a missed connection are not noted. Some clients log in from places that are not private.
- cost: Without a record, nothing can be shown of how and when sessions were held.
- fix: Each session records its mode, the consent for online sessions and a note that the client confirmed they could talk privately. Missed connections are recorded as such.
- screen: sessions

### 10. Fees, packages and receipts
- area: Money
- scene: A client has paid for a package of eight sessions and attended five. Another has an outstanding amount from August. A sliding-scale fee is agreed verbally with one client and forgotten by the next clinician.
- cost: Example arithmetic: ₹3,000 outstanding across ten clients is ₹30,000 a quarter that nobody is chasing because it is uncomfortable.
- fix: Each client has an agreed fee, a package balance and dues. Session attendance reduces the package, and outstanding amounts show to the coordinator, so the conversation is calm and informed. Verity does not take payments.
- screen: money

### 11. Clinician credentials and supervision
- area: People
- scene: A clinician's registration renewal date is in an email. A trainee needs supervision hours logged. A new counsellor joins and her qualification certificate is on a phone.
- cost: Practising without valid registration where it is required is a legal and reputational risk. Clinical psychologists must be enrolled in the Central Rehabilitation Register, whose current norms took effect on 14 October 2025.
- fix: Each clinician has a record with qualifications, registration and its dates, supervision schedule and training. Verity raises tasks before dates lapse, and shows which clinician is cleared for which service.
- screen: team
- source: (Rehabilitation Council of India norms, 2025: https://rehabcouncil.nic.in/norms-guidelines/)

### 12. Referrals in and out
- area: Referrals
- scene: A psychiatrist refers a client for therapy and expects an update. A clinician refers a client for medication review and cannot say whether the client went.
- cost: Referral loops that never close mean a client can fall through the gap between professionals.
- fix: A referral is a record with the sender, receiver, client consent to share and a status. The clinician decides what information goes across. Verity tracks whether the loop closed.
- screen: clients

### 13. The practice owner's blind spot
- area: Owner
- scene: The owner wants to know how many sessions were delivered, how many were cancelled late, which clinicians have open slots, and what is outstanding. Each clinician keeps their own sheet.
- cost: Without a shared view the practice cannot plan capacity or know whether to take on another clinician.
- fix: Sessions, cancellations, clinician availability, dues and follow-ups appear in one view, with no client content. A second centre is a location that rolls up.
- screen: owner

## Roles

Heading: Everyone sees the same records, through their own window.

- Accountant: What was earned and what is owed? | Fees collected, packages and dues, without any client detail beyond an ID.

## FAQ

Heading: Questions practices ask.

- **Can the front desk see what is said in sessions?** No. The desk sees appointments, rooms and fees. Notes are restricted to the clinician who wrote them, and anyone that clinician allows. Every open of a record is logged.
- **Does Verity read my notes or use them for anything?** Verity AI works from schedule, follow-up and fee records. It does not read session notes, and it does not give clinical advice.
- **Do I have to put notes into Verity?** No. Notes can stay where you keep them now. If you choose to hold them in Verity, they are restricted documents with the clinician as the only reader unless shared.
- **How is a client's consent recorded?** Intake details and each consent, such as treatment, online sessions, or sharing with a family member or referrer, are dated records with the version agreed. A withdrawal is recorded and visible to those who need to act on it.
- **Can it help with a client's request to see their records?** It tracks the request as a task with a due date, and the clinician records the decision, including any withheld portion. Verity does not decide what to release.
- **Can it send reminders to clients?** Not on its own. Verity lists who needs a check-in and records the outcome. Staff or the clinician reach out.
- **I am a solo counsellor. Is this too much?** A solo practice has the same cancellations and follow-ups and fewer hours to chase them. We set up only what you need in the same four weeks.
- **We have several clinicians and two centres. Can we see them together?** Yes, without client content. Sessions, open slots and dues roll up by centre and clinician.
- **What does it cost?** It depends on the number of clinicians, centres and what you want set up. Message us and we will give you a clear number after a short conversation about how your practice runs.
