## How it runs today

**Most interior design firms win projects on taste and lose margin on coordination.**

- The design is the visible part: the mood board, the 3D views, the material samples, the final room. What is hard to see is the paperwork around it: which version the client signed off, which BOQ line has gone over, which vendor is late and what that does to the carpenter's slot, and whether the second payment is due.
- That information lives in many places. Approvals are in chat threads and in a signed PDF. The BOQ is a spreadsheet that gets copied for each revision. Vendor quotes are in email. Site updates are photos in a WhatsApp group. Payment stages are in the quotation.
- A 2026 client-side guide on turnkey interiors tells homeowners never to sign without a BOQ that shows brand, model, unit price and quantity for every line, and to tie each payment to a physical milestone. Clients are being coached to ask for exactly the detail that many studios keep in their heads.
- runs on: Design software and shared folders; Excel for BOQ and budget; WhatsApp groups with clients, vendors and site teams; Quotation and agreement PDFs; Accounting software kept by the accountant
- stack note: Each one is useful. None of them tells you that the client approved the wardrobe finish on the 12th, the laminate was ordered on the 14th, and the BOQ still shows the old rate.

## Pains

Heading: Where an interior design practice actually loses money and time.
Lede: These are the situations studio owners, designers and project managers describe again and again. For each one: what it looks like on an ordinary week, what it costs, and what Verity does about it.

### 1. Clients approve by chat and later say they never did
- area: Approvals
- scene: The client sent a thumbs-up on the kitchen layout in a group chat. Three weeks later, with the carcass made, they say they wanted the sink on the other wall. The message is somewhere in 600 others.
- cost: Rework on joinery that has already been made is paid for by the studio unless the approval is clear. Example arithmetic: a rebuilt modular kitchen carcass costing ₹1.2 lakh, half of it wasted, is ₹60,000 against a design fee that was perhaps ₹1.5 lakh.
- fix: Each design item, such as a layout, material or finish, is a record with versions, the client's approval, the date and the person who gave it. Work orders to vendors can start only from an approved item, and the approved version is the one the vendor sees.
- screen: approvals

### 2. Revision rounds go past what was agreed
- area: Approvals
- scene: The agreement covers three design rounds. The client is in round six for the living room. No one has said so.
- cost: A published guide for homeowners suggests that more than two to three revision cycles be treated as excluded and priced separately. Rounds that are not counted are rounds that are not charged.
- fix: Every round of changes is logged against the room with the date and hours spent. When the agreed number is reached, the next round creates a task for the lead designer to quote an extra, and the client's approval is recorded before design work resumes.
- screen: approvals
- source: (Studio Matrx, Turnkey interiors in India, 2026: https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/turnkey-interiors-india)

### 3. Selections are late and hold up the whole schedule
- area: Approvals
- scene: Tiles, sanitaryware and lighting must be chosen before the civil work ends. The client takes three weeks to decide. The carpenter's slot is booked for the next Monday.
- cost: A late selection delays everything after it, and the delay is usually blamed on the studio, not the client.
- fix: Each selection has a needed-by date tied to a site stage. Open ones are flagged before the stage starts, and the task goes to the designer who deals with the client, with the impact on the schedule shown.
- screen: approvals

### 4. The BOQ in the quote and the BOQ in reality drift apart
- area: Budget
- scene: The quotation was ₹38 lakh. Plywood prices rose, the client upgraded the wardrobe finish and the lights were changed. Nobody has updated the BOQ. At handover the studio's own cost is ₹3 lakh more than the budget.
- cost: Example arithmetic: a 6 percent drift on a ₹38 lakh job is ₹2.3 lakh. Studios usually find it at handover, when it can no longer be passed on.
- fix: The BOQ is a record by line with quantity, brand, unit rate and budget. Each purchase and vendor bill is recorded against its line, and Verity shows budget, committed and actual per line and per room, flagging lines going over.
- screen: boq

### 5. Variations and extras are agreed in passing
- area: Budget
- scene: The client asks for a niche in the passage and a changed ceiling in the study during a site visit. Both are done the next week. The variation is not priced.
- cost: Extras that are done and not billed cost the studio materials, labour and goodwill when it is finally raised.
- fix: A variation is a record with the request, the estimate and the client's approval. Work is marked uninstructed until approved, and approved variations add to the BOQ and to the next payment stage.
- screen: boq

### 6. Material rates change after the quote
- area: Budget
- scene: The quote was priced in July. By October laminates, hardware and plywood cost more. The studio had promised a fixed price. The rate difference is absorbed.
- cost: A fixed price in a rising market shrinks the margin purchase by purchase. A client guide in 2026 suggests capping rate escalation at 5 percent or fixing the price in writing.
- fix: Each purchase records the rate against the BOQ rate. Verity flags lines where the rate paid is above the one quoted, with the quantity still to buy, so you can ask the client for a revision or change the specification while it is still possible.
- screen: boq
- source: (Studio Matrx, Turnkey interiors in India, 2026: https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/turnkey-interiors-india)

### 7. Vendors are late and the site waits
- area: Vendors
- scene: The sofa is delayed by two weeks, the stone for the counter has not been cut, and the electrician has gone to another site. The designer rearranges the plan by phone for the third time.
- cost: Every day of overrun is a day of rent for the client and a day of site cost for the studio, plus an unhappy customer.
- fix: Each order has a vendor, an expected date and a promised date, with the site stage that depends on it. Verity lists orders at risk with the stage they will hold up, and records the vendor's reply and new date.
- screen: site

### 8. Vendor quotes, orders and payments are scattered
- area: Vendors
- scene: The carpenter's quote is on email, the order on WhatsApp, the advance by UPI and the bill on paper. At month end nobody can say how much has been paid for which project.
- cost: Duplicated payments, advances forgotten and disputes with vendors.
- fix: A vendor order is a record with the project, BOQ line, quote, advance paid, delivery and bill. Payments are made against orders, and the studio sees what is owed to each vendor and for which project.
- screen: site

### 9. Site stages overlap and trades collide
- area: Site
- scene: The painters arrive while the false ceiling team is still working. The polishing team finds the floor not protected. The site supervisor sorts it by shouting.
- cost: Rework and damage to finished surfaces, and a schedule that slips by days each time.
- fix: The site has a stage plan with the trades and dates, shown on one schedule. Overlaps are flagged and the site supervisor sees what is next for each trade, with the dependency on materials and approvals.
- screen: site

### 10. Site updates are photos nobody can search
- area: Site
- scene: The client asks what the site looked like before the false ceiling. The designer scrolls through a WhatsApp group of 1,800 photos.
- cost: Hours lost searching, and a weaker position in any later dispute about what was done and when.
- fix: Site visits are records with date, room, photos and notes. Issues raised become tasks for a trade with a due date, and a client update can be put together from the record.
- screen: site

### 11. Payments are asked for by date, not by milestone
- area: Money
- scene: The second instalment is due when the carpentry is installed. The studio realises it two weeks after the carpentry is finished. The client has already moved to the next request.
- cost: Example arithmetic: a 30 percent stage on a ₹38 lakh job is ₹11.4 lakh. Two weeks late on a studio's own borrowed cash is a real cost, and a stage not asked for in time is often paid in parts.
- fix: The payment schedule is a set of stages tied to site milestones. When a milestone is marked done, a payment request task goes to accounts with the amount and the evidence, and the stage stays red on the owner's screen until paid.
- screen: payments

### 12. Advances are large and the work is thin
- area: Money
- scene: A studio takes 50 percent upfront for a fit-out because the client agrees. Work starts slowly. The client asks for a refund after four weeks and the studio has already paid vendor advances.
- cost: A published guide notes that capping the upfront payment at 20 to 25 percent and tying later payments to physical milestones is the usual advice to clients. Studios that collect more take on more risk. In 2026 a lawyer's article described stalled interior jobs with large advances ending in consumer complaints.
- fix: The schedule shows advance, milestones and final retention. Verity compares money collected with work completed and vendor money committed, and flags projects where the studio holds less than it has committed.
- screen: payments
- source: (Sudhir Rao, Advocate, Interior designer abandons project after 80% advance, 2026: https://sudhirrao.com/insights/interior-designer-abandons-project-after-80-advance-legal-re)

### 13. Snags and the final payment fight each other
- area: Handover
- scene: At handover the client lists forty snags: a scratched panel, a loose handle, a mismatched paint patch. The final payment of 10 percent is held until all are done. Weeks pass.
- cost: Final payments held for months, and a client who tells friends the studio does not finish. Retention is normal; open-ended retention is not.
- fix: Each snag is a record with photo, room, trade, owner and due date. The client's sign-off on each is recorded, and the final payment task is tied to the list so both sides can see what is left.
- screen: handover
- source: (Studio Matrx, Turnkey interiors in India, 2026: https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/turnkey-interiors-india)

### 14. Complaints after handover have no history
- area: Handover
- scene: Six months later a drawer sags and the paint peels near a window. The project manager has left. The client calls the designer and nobody knows who made what.
- cost: A complaint that is handled badly can become a legal one. Stalled or deficient interior work can be taken to a consumer commission as deficiency in service.
- fix: Handover closes the project but keeps its full record: approvals, BOQ, vendors, site photos and snags. A defect call is logged on the project, goes to the vendor who made that item, and has a due date.
- screen: handover

### 15. Enquiries and quotes go cold
- area: New work
- scene: A couple visits the studio, likes the portfolio and asks for a quote. The quote takes ten days. They sign with another studio that replied in two.
- cost: A project is won on responsiveness as much as on taste. Each quote is hours of designer time that is wasted when it is not followed up.
- fix: Each enquiry is a record with source, site, budget, scope and next step. Verity lists quotes sent and not followed up, and records the outcome and the reason for loss.
- screen: owner

### 16. The owner cannot see the whole studio
- area: Owner
- scene: The principal moves between a site, a vendor showroom and a client meeting. Twelve projects run in parallel and each designer manages four.
- cost: A designer overloaded on four projects makes a mistake on one. The owner finds out at the complaint.
- fix: One owner view shows each project's stage, approvals pending, BOQ drift, vendor delays, payment stages due and each designer's load, with the person responsible and the next step.
- screen: owner

## Rules and paperwork

Note: Rules change and differ by state. Confirm current requirements with your accountant and your lawyer.

- **GST on design and execution**: what: Interior design consultancy and interior work with materials and labour are generally taxed at 18 percent, and composite interior work is generally treated as a works contract. Whether you must register depends on your turnover and state, and the classification of each job is for your accountant. | how: Each project records the treatment your accountant sets, and design fees and execution invoices are kept as separate lines so the client sees what each covers. - **Consumer protection**: what: A consumer can complain about deficient or delayed service under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019. A lawyer's 2026 article describes interior jobs that stalled after large advances being taken to the District Commission, which generally hears claims up to Rs 50 lakh. | how: The approvals, BOQ, vendor orders, site photos and payments for each project are kept with dates, so the studio can show what was agreed and delivered. - **Written scope, BOQ and payment terms**: what: Disputes in interiors generally come down to what was agreed. A 2026 guide for clients advises a BOQ with brand, model, unit price and quantity per line, revision cycles defined in writing, and payments tied to physical milestones. ## FAQ

Heading: Questions interior design firms ask.

- **Does Verity replace my design and rendering software?** No. You keep designing where you design. Verity records the approvals, versions, BOQ, vendor orders, site work and payments around the design.
- **We are a four-person studio. Is this too much?** The same approvals, BOQ drift and payment stages need tracking at any size. We set up only what you need in the same four weeks and leave out the rest.
- **Can clients approve designs inside Verity?** Approval is recorded by your team against the item, with the client's sign-off attached, such as a signed PDF or a message. Clients do not log in unless you decide so during setup.
- **Can it keep my BOQ and show what I actually spent?** Yes. Each line has a budget and the purchases and bills recorded against it, so committed and actual cost show per line and per room.
- **Does it place orders with my vendors?** No. Your team places orders. Verity records each one with the promised date, the BOQ line and the stage it holds, and flags those at risk.
- **How do you handle projects already half done?** We bring each live project in at its current stage with the latest approvals, the BOQ as it stands, open vendor orders and the payment stages already paid. Finished projects can come later as archives.
- **Can it help with the final payment and snags?** Yes. Each snag has a trade, a due date and the client's sign-off, and the final payment task is tied to the list. The client still decides when to pay.
- **I manage projects for architects and builders too. Is that different?** The records are the same with different parties. Each project has its own approval steps and payment terms.
- **Who can see my data?** Only the people you allow. Designers see their projects, vendors see their own orders, and the principal and accountant see money. Every change is recorded with who made it and when.
- **What does it cost?** It depends on the number of people, projects and what you want set up. Message us and we will give you a clear number after a short conversation about how your studio runs.
