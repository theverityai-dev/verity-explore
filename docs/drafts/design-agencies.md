## How it runs today

**The design is organised. The business around it is held together by chat threads.**

- Inside a design tool, everything is tidy: layers, artboards, versions. Outside it, the studio runs on a client WhatsApp group, a shared drive with folders named by whoever created them, a timesheet someone fills on Friday from memory, and a quote that was a PDF three months ago.
- So the questions that decide whether a studio makes money have no single answer. Which round of changes are we on? Did the client approve the logo, or did someone just say it looked fine on a call? Which designer is free next week? Who owns the font that went into the final files, and for how long is the stock image licensed?
- The studio founder usually answers these from memory, between creative reviews. It works while there are six projects. At fifteen, work still gets done but margin, deadlines and ownership of the final files quietly get worse.
- runs on: Client WhatsApp groups; Shared drive and design files; Spreadsheet timesheet; Quote and invoice PDFs; Email threads; Founder’s memory
- stack note: Each tool is fine at its own job. Nothing ties a feedback message to a round, a round to hours, or hours to a margin.

## Pains

Heading: Where a design studio actually loses money and time.
Lede: These are the situations studio founders describe. For each one: what it looks like on an ordinary week, what it costs, and what Verity does about it.

### 1. Revision rounds run past what was quoted
- area: Scope
- scene: The quote said two rounds of changes. The client is now on round five, each one small, each one polite. Nobody wants to be the person who raises it, so the designer just does it.
- cost: Example arithmetic: a ₹1.5 lakh brand identity quoted at 60 hours that takes 95 has lost about ₹55,000 of time at a ₹1,500 hourly cost rate, and the studio never saw the line item.
- fix: Each project record holds the rounds included in the quote. Every client feedback is logged as a numbered round. When the count passes the allowance, the project manager gets a flag with the hours used so far, so the extra work is quoted or agreed rather than absorbed.
- screen: project

### 2. Feedback is scattered across chats, calls and email
- area: Scope
- scene: Changes arrive as a voice note, a screenshot with a red circle, a line in an email and something said on a call. The designer works from whichever she saw last. Two weeks later nobody can say what was actually asked for.
- cost: Rework, missed changes and arguments about who said what. A studio that cannot show what was requested cannot show it did what was asked.
- fix: Feedback is attached to the project as a round, with the sender, the date, the list of changes and the files. The designer works from one checklist per round, and ticks off each change as it is done.
- screen: project

### 3. Approvals are verbal, and projects stall waiting for them
- area: Clients
- scene: The client said “looks good” on a call, then goes quiet for ten days. The launch date was theirs, but the delay lands on the studio. When the invoice goes out, they say they never approved the final.
- cost: Idle designers, a project that stays open on the books, and a delayed final payment tied to a sign-off that was never recorded.
- fix: Each deliverable has an approval state: with studio, with client, approved, or changes requested. Anything waiting on the client longer than your set number of days appears on a list with a reminder task for the account manager, and the approval is recorded with who gave it and when.
- screen: approvals

### 4. Unpaid pitches eat a week and win nothing
- area: Pitches
- scene: A prospect asks for “just a few ideas” before they decide. Two designers spend three days on concepts. The prospect picks another studio, or never replies.
- cost: Example arithmetic: three pitches a month at 24 designer hours each, at a ₹1,500 cost per hour, is ₹1.08 lakh of unpaid work every month. Few studios know the number or how many converted.
- fix: A pitch is a record with the prospect, the hours spent and the outcome. Over a few months the pipeline screen shows hours spent per pitch won, so the studio can decide which pitches to decline, charge a pitch fee for, or limit to a fixed number of hours.
- screen: pipeline

### 5. Enquiries and proposals go cold
- area: Clients
- scene: A referral sends a message on Monday. The proposal is promised for Thursday and goes out the following Tuesday. The client has already spoken to two others.
- cost: The best leads in a design studio are referrals, and they are the ones who expect a fast, clear reply. A slow reply is read as a slow studio.
- fix: Every enquiry becomes a record with an owner and a next step. Proposals past their promised date, and sent proposals with no reply after your set interval, appear on the pipeline list as tasks for the person who owns them.
- screen: pipeline

### 6. Stock images, fonts and music licences are forgotten
- area: Licences
- scene: The designer bought a licensed font for one project, then used it on the next. A stock image was licensed for web only and ended up on a hoarding. The licence on a music track expired last month and the video is still live.
- cost: A claim from the rights holder, a forced takedown, or an unplanned cost when a client’s lawyer asks where the licence is. Rarely large, always unpleasant, and fully avoidable.
- fix: Every licensed asset is a record: what it is, who bought it, for which project, which uses and territory, and until when. Final deliverables list the licences they depend on. Licences about to expire, or used beyond their listed use, show up on a screen.
- screen: licences

### 7. Nobody can say what the client owns when the project ends
- area: Handover
- scene: The client asks for the source files two years later. The designer has left. The agreement said “all rights” on one page and “licence to use” on another. The final files are on someone’s laptop.
- cost: Disputes over ownership, or a studio forced to rebuild old work for free. Under Indian copyright law, a transfer of rights generally has to be in writing and signed, so what was agreed on a call does not settle it.
- fix: Each project keeps its signed agreement, the list of deliverables handed over, the date, and who received them. Source files and final exports are attached to the project record, not to a designer’s laptop.
- screen: project
- source: (Bhatt & Joshi Associates, on assignment under the Copyright Act: https://bhattandjoshiassociates.com/copyright-assignment-licensing-in-india-key-clauses-term-limits-enforcement/)

### 8. One designer is overbooked while another has nothing
- area: People
- scene: The senior designer is on four projects and every one is late. A junior has a free week that nobody planned for, because no one can see who is on what.
- cost: Missed deadlines on one side and paid idle time on the other. The studio hires more people when it may only need to spread the work.
- fix: Each project assigns designers by week. The capacity screen shows booked hours against available hours for each designer, and flags anyone over their limit, so work is moved before the deadline is missed.
- screen: capacity

### 9. You find out a project lost money after it is over
- area: Money
- scene: The quote was ₹2.4 lakh. Hours were logged on Fridays from memory, expenses sit in a different sheet, and a freelancer’s invoice arrives a month later. By then the project is closed.
- cost: Pricing stays a guess, the same loss-making kind of project gets taken again, and the studio cannot tell its best clients from its most expensive ones.
- fix: Quoted fee, hours logged, freelancer cost and expenses sit on the same project. A running margin shows while the work is live, and a project that crosses 80 percent of its budget before reaching the final stage is flagged.
- screen: studio

### 10. Milestone invoices are raised late, and paid later
- area: Money
- scene: The second milestone was due after concept approval. The approval happened on the 3rd, the invoice went out on the 19th, and payment came in the following month. Meanwhile salaries were paid on time.
- cost: A studio funds its clients’ delays. Under the MSMED Act, payments to micro and small suppliers are due within a written term not exceeding 45 days, but the studio has to have invoiced on time and be able to show it.
- fix: Each milestone is tied to a deliverable state. When the approval is recorded, a task is created to raise the invoice. Overdue invoices appear by client with age, and the follow-up call is a task with an owner.
- screen: pipeline
- source: (Taxmann, on MSMED Act section 15 and section 43B(h): https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/analysis-practical-insights-into-section-43bh-regarding-disallowance-of-delayed-payments-to-mses/)

### 11. Retainer clients quietly use more than they pay for
- area: Clients
- scene: A monthly retainer covers 40 hours of social and campaign design. Small requests keep arriving by message, and the real usage is 65 hours. The retainer has not been repriced in two years.
- cost: Example arithmetic: 25 extra hours a month at a ₹1,500 cost per hour is ₹37,500 a month, or ₹4.5 lakh a year, given away on one client.
- fix: A retainer is a record with its monthly hours and scope. Every request is logged against it, and the balance is visible to the account manager. When usage crosses the monthly allowance, the account manager is prompted to raise it with the client.
- screen: capacity

### 12. Freelancers hold files, briefs and know-how that leave with them
- area: People
- scene: A freelance illustrator did half the identity work. The files are on a personal drive, the brief was a voice note, and the invoice arrives without a reference to any project.
- cost: Lost files, unclear rights over what the freelancer produced, and invoices that cannot be matched to a project’s cost.
- fix: Freelancers are recorded as people with their rate, projects and agreement. Their files are attached to the project record and their invoices are matched to a project, so cost and ownership are clear.
- screen: capacity

### 13. Past work and brand knowledge cannot be found
- area: Owner
- scene: A returning client asks for the layout used for their brochure last year. The designer who made it has moved on. Someone searches four folders and three chat threads and rebuilds it.
- cost: The same work done twice, and a client who wonders why a studio they have worked with for three years has no memory of them.
- fix: Every client keeps a history: projects, rounds, approved files, brand notes and licences used. Anyone on the team opens the client and sees what was done, when, and by whom. Verity AI can answer a question about it in plain words.
- screen: studio

## Day, before and after

Heading: The same Wednesday in a studio, before and after.
Lede: The creative work does not change. What changes is that each message, round and hour lands on the project, so nobody reconstructs it later.

- Morning: before: The account manager scrolls through six client chats to find out which changes are still pending. | after: The project screen lists open changes for each round. She starts from the checklist, not from the chats.
- Late morning: before: A client sends a fifth round of feedback by voice note. The designer does it without saying anything. | after: The round is logged and the project flags that it is past the two quoted. The account manager raises the extra with the client the same day.
- Midday: before: A prospect asks for a few concepts. You say yes because it is easier than saying no. | after: You see this studio has spent 70 pitch hours this quarter and won one. You offer a paid concept round instead.
- Afternoon: before: The art director finds out on Thursday that three projects all need her on Friday. | after: The capacity screen showed her at 100 percent on Monday. A junior picked up two tasks before anything slipped.
- Evening: before: A client approved the final over a call. Nobody raises the invoice for two weeks. | after: The approval is recorded, and a task to raise the milestone invoice lands on finance the same evening.
- Month end: before: You discover from the books that two projects lost money. | after: Both were flagged at 80 percent of budget. One was repriced, one was closed with an extra charge agreed.

## How we work

Heading: We set it up with you, in four weeks.
Lede: You do not get a login and a manual. We learn how a project moves through your studio, from brief to handover, then build Verity around it and stay on to make it work.

### Week 1: Discovery and mapping
- we: Sit with you and your lead designer or account manager. Follow two live projects from brief to final files, and see where feedback, hours and approvals are kept today.
- you: Two to three hours of your time and an hour from the account manager. Access to a recent quote, a timesheet, and the folders and chats of two projects.
- get: A written map of how a project runs in your studio, your service lines, rates, stages, and the five problems we will fix first.

### Weeks 2 to 3: Configuration
- we: Set up your project stages, quoted rounds, hour budgets, approval states, retainer rules, licence records and invoice milestones. Build the screens around your own words.
- you: Check the setup with your lead designer. Tell us where a stage or a rule is wrong.
- get: A working Verity with your clients, designers and project types, ready for your live projects.

### Weeks 3 to 4: Migration and training
- we: Bring in your client list, open projects, retainers and licence records from your sheets, and attach the key files and agreements. Train designers in short sessions on their own projects.
- you: Your client sheet, current quotes and a list of live projects. Thirty minutes with each designer.
- get: Every live project inside Verity with its rounds, hours and approvals, and a team that has logged a real round.

### After go-live: Operations partnership
- we: Stay on after launch. Review the first month of projects with you, tune the flags, and handle changes as your service lines change.
- you: A short monthly review of margin, pitches and overdue invoices.
- get: A system that follows how the studio sells and delivers, with someone responsible for it.

- bring: Two or three recent quotes or proposals; Your client list, even if it is a sheet or a phone book; The timesheet or hours tracker you use now, if any; Your standard client agreement and any freelancer agreement; Your list of licensed fonts, stock accounts and music subscriptions
- time: About 8 to 10 hours of your own time across the four weeks, mostly in week 1. The account manager needs a few more, and each designer about half an hour.
- after: Nobody is hired to run Verity. Rounds, hours and approvals are logged as the work happens, and the reports come from those records.

## Rules and paperwork

Heading: The rules a design studio has to keep up with.
Lede: Verity does not give legal or tax advice and does not file anything for you. It keeps the records these rules depend on in order, so your adviser is not rebuilding them from chat threads.
Note: Rules change and differ by case. Confirm current requirements with your accountant or lawyer. Verity does not file anything.

- **Copyright assignment and licences**: what: Under the Copyright Act, an assignment of copyright has to be in writing and signed. If the deed does not state a period it is generally treated as five years, and if it states no territory it is presumed to cover India. Third-party fonts, images and music carry their own licence terms. | how: The signed agreement, the list of what was handed over, and every third-party licence sit on the project record, with dates and permitted uses. (Bhatt & Joshi Associates: https://bhattandjoshiassociates.com/copyright-assignment-licensing-in-india-key-clauses-term-limits-enforcement/)
- **Timely payment of micro and small suppliers**: what: The MSMED Act sets payment within the agreed written term, not more than 45 days, or 15 days with no written agreement. Section 43B(h) of the Income Tax Act, from assessment year 2024-25, links a buyer’s tax deduction to paying micro and small suppliers on time. This matters to you both as a supplier owed money and as a buyer of freelancer services if they are micro or small enterprises. | how: Invoice dates, agreed credit terms and payment dates are recorded for client invoices and for freelancer and vendor bills, so ageing is visible. (Taxmann: https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/analysis-practical-insights-into-section-43bh-regarding-disallowance-of-delayed-payments-to-mses/)
- **GST on design services**: what: Design services are generally charged GST at 18 percent. Work for a client outside India, paid in foreign currency, may qualify as an export of services, which is generally zero rated under a Letter of Undertaking. Check the current rate and conditions before invoicing. | how: Each invoice records the client, place and type of service, so your accountant can separate domestic and export work at month end. (Taxaj guide on GST for graphic design: https://www.taxaj.com/learn/gst-applicability-tax-rates-on-graphic-design-services/)
- **Client and personal data**: what: Client contact details, staff details and any end-customer data in a project are personal data. India’s data protection rules are being phased in, so keep access narrow and know where data sits. | how: Client data lives in one permissioned place, and who viewed or changed a record is logged, instead of sitting in personal chat apps.

## FAQ

Heading: Questions studio founders ask.

- **Will my designers actually use it?** Designers only touch what concerns them: the checklist for the current round, their hours, and their projects. We keep it to a few clicks and train on a live project, not in a classroom. They also get something back: a clear list instead of a stack of chat messages.
- **Does Verity replace Figma, Adobe or my drive?** No. Designers keep working in their tools and your files stay where they are. Verity keeps the business side of each project: the quote, rounds, hours, approvals, licences and invoices, with a pointer to the final files.
- **Can it track revision rounds without annoying my clients?** Clients do not need to use Verity. Your account manager logs feedback as a round when it arrives, from whichever channel it came on. The client sees the same polite process, and you get the numbers.
- **We are a small studio with eight people. Is this too much?** The leaks are the same at eight people, with less spare time to notice them. We set up only what you need, and the four weeks run the same way.
- **Can it handle freelancers and agencies we subcontract to?** Yes. Freelancers are recorded with their rate and agreement, linked to the projects they work on, and see only those projects. Their bills are matched to a project, so project margin includes them.
- **Does it track image, font and music licences?** It records them as assets with the project, permitted use, territory and expiry, and flags the ones about to end or used beyond their terms. It does not check licence terms against the law, so keep your own copies of what was bought.
- **Who can see my client data and my margins?** Only the people you allow. Access is set by role, so designers, account managers, finance and freelancers each see only what they need, and every change is recorded with who made it and when.
- **How long before I see something useful?** The revision flag, approval list and capacity view need only your live projects and quotes, so they are the first things we aim to have working. Margin and pitch conversion become reliable after a few weeks of logged hours.
- **What does it cost?** It depends on the size of your team and what you want set up. Message us and we will give you a clear number after a short conversation about how your studio runs.
