## How it runs today

**A venue runs on a diary, a cash box and the owner’s memory.**

- A family visits on a Sunday, likes the lawn, and pays a token to “hold the date”. A second family calls a week later for the same date and is told it is free, because the hold was never written on the main diary. The error is found when both turn up with their wedding cards printed.
- On the day itself, the caterer, decorator, DJ and your own staff all work from different information. The final guest count was agreed on a call, the menu changed twice by message, and the extra hour that the DJ played was never charged. At the end, the damage to a door and a dozen missing steel plates is argued over with nothing written down.
- Most of the year the hall is full for about 45 days of peak wedding season and much emptier outside it. A venue cannot make up for an unsold date, so the owner’s main questions are always about dates: which are open, which are held, which are at risk, and what has been collected against each.
- runs on: Paper diary or wall calendar; Cash and UPI advances in several hands; WhatsApp with clients and vendors; Excel sheet of bookings; Owner’s memory of who was promised what
- stack note: Each of these works for one job. None of them tells you the true state of a date.

## Pains

Heading: Where an event venue actually loses money.
Lede: These are the situations venue owners describe again and again. For each one: what it looks like on a normal week, what it costs, and what Verity does about it.

### 1. The same date is sold twice
- area: Dates
- scene: The sales manager noted a hold for 12 December in her own diary. The owner confirmed the date to another family from the front desk. Nobody saw the clash until the second family paid an advance.
- cost: A refund, an angry family and the word that goes round a close-knit community. Trade writers on Indian banquets point to the lack of simple booking software as the cause of double booking.
- fix: Every hall, lawn and slot is one calendar. A date is open, held or confirmed, and a booking cannot be confirmed on a date that is already taken. Everyone on the team looks at the same calendar.
- screen: calendar

### 2. Pencil holds that never expire
- area: Dates
- scene: A hold for a good Saturday has been sitting for five weeks on a promise to “confirm after the muhurat”. Three other families were told the date was gone.
- cost: Example arithmetic: a Saturday hall rental of ₹3 lakh turned away three times for a hold that was never paid is a ₹3 lakh date lost, and the other families booked elsewhere.
- fix: A hold carries an expiry date and an amount if one is paid. Verity lists holds about to expire, with the family and the person handling them, and releases the date back to the calendar when the hold lapses.
- screen: calendar

### 3. Site visits that never get a second call
- area: Enquiries
- scene: A couple walks through on a Sunday, takes the brochure and says they will think about it. The sales team is busy with the next family, and nobody calls them back the following week.
- cost: Example arithmetic: 30 visits a month and a 25 percent close rate at ₹2.5 lakh per event. Lifting the close rate to 30 percent adds 1.5 events a month, about ₹45 lakh a year.
- fix: Each visit becomes an enquiry with the date they want, guest count, budget and what they liked. Verity sets the follow-up task and lists enquiries that have gone quiet, with the salesperson who owns each.
- screen: enquiries

### 4. Advances and instalments are collected in several hands
- area: Money
- scene: A token was taken in cash by the manager, a second part by UPI to the owner’s phone, and a cheque is waiting at the front desk. The family says they have paid ₹2 lakh. The books show ₹1.5 lakh.
- cost: Money that cannot be traced to a booking, disputes about what was paid and an advance that gets spent before the event happens.
- fix: Every payment, cash, cheque or UPI, is recorded against the booking with date, mode and who took it. The balance is always visible, and the payment schedule shows what is still due before the event.
- screen: payments

### 5. Cancellation and refund terms are not written at the time of booking
- area: Money
- scene: A wedding is called off. The family expects a full refund because the owner said it was “flexible”. The owner remembers a different arrangement. A consumer group takes up the matter.
- cost: Refund disputes, goodwill lost and sometimes a legal notice. Consumer reports on halls often describe cancellation terms that were never explained at booking.
- fix: The booking records its cancellation and refund terms, and the date from which each applies. A cancellation then shows exactly what is retained and what is returned, with the decision recorded.
- screen: event

### 6. What sales promised does not reach the people running the event
- area: Events
- scene: The family was promised the stage on the north side, an extra generator and a separate green room. The event manager on the day knows none of this, and the family’s father finds out at 6 pm.
- cost: Free fixes made on the spot, bad reviews and staff who are blamed for a promise they never heard.
- fix: The event has one order sheet with the hall layout, menu, timings, extras and special requests, agreed with the family and shared with the event manager, the caterer and the decorator. Changes go on the same page.
- screen: event

### 7. Guest count agreed on a call, billed from memory
- area: Events
- scene: The family said 500 guests. The caterer was told 550. On the evening, 620 plates are served. Nobody has a written number to bill against.
- cost: Under-recovered catering cost, or an argument on the last day that sours an otherwise good event.
- fix: The expected guest count is recorded with dates, along with the final guarantee given before the event. The actual count served is entered on the day, and the difference is billed from the record.
- screen: event

### 8. Extra hours, extra tables and extras that are never charged
- area: Events
- scene: The DJ plays for an extra hour after the agreed cut-off. A second generator is run. Extra chairs are added for 80 more guests. The manager mentions it at closing but the bill goes out unchanged.
- cost: Example arithmetic: ₹15,000 of uncharged extras on each of 5 events a month is ₹9 lakh a year in income lost.
- fix: Extras are logged against the event on the day, with time and quantity, and added to the final bill. Overtime is flagged against the agreed end time.
- screen: dayof

### 9. Outside vendors turn up without a plan
- area: Events
- scene: The decorator needs access from 6 am, the caterer’s trucks need the loading bay at the same time, and the sound company brings equipment you did not know about. The gate has no list.
- cost: Delays that push the event start, access disputes and damage to the hall by people you have no record of.
- fix: Each outside vendor is a record on the event with their arrival time, access need and a contact. The day-of checklist lists who is due when, and vendor deposits and rules are attached.
- screen: dayof

### 10. Damage and missing items are argued over after the event
- area: Assets
- scene: A glass panel is cracked and 40 steel plates are missing. The family says it was already damaged. You hold a security deposit but have no before-and-after record.
- cost: Either an unreimbursed repair or an argument that ends the relationship with the family and their circle.
- fix: A handover checklist is done before and after the event with photos and counts of key items, signed off by both sides. The deposit is settled from the record, and damage is tied to the event.
- screen: assets

### 11. Crockery, linen and furniture go missing between events
- area: Assets
- scene: The store keeper counted 800 plates in March. In October, after a busy season, a count shows 690. Nobody knows which event lost them.
- cost: Replacement cost that repeats every season and a store that is never trusted to be accurate.
- fix: Counts of key items are taken in and out for each event, so losses are tied to an event and a handover. The stock list shows what is short before the next big booking.
- screen: assets

### 12. Extra staff for an event are arranged and paid by guess
- area: Staff
- scene: A 600-guest wedding needs 25 casual workers. The manager calls contacts, a few do not show, and wages are paid in cash from the evening’s advance without a record of who worked.
- cost: Understaffed events, wage disputes, and a cost per event that nobody can state afterwards.
- fix: Each event has a staffing plan by role and shift. Attendance is marked on the day, and the wage cost per event is calculated from it. Verity does not run payroll.
- screen: dayof

### 13. Licences and clearances expire unnoticed
- area: Compliance
- scene: The fire clearance lapsed last month. The food licence is due next week. A local notice about loudspeaker timings sits on the owner’s desk. Reports from several towns show halls run without valid trade licences.
- cost: Fines, a closure order on a peak weekend, and loss of insurance cover if there is an incident.
- fix: Each licence, clearance and insurance policy is a record with its expiry date, and Verity lists what runs out in the next 60 days with a task for the person responsible.
- screen: assets

### 14. Idle weekdays and the off-season are never planned
- area: Owner
- scene: December is full. March to May is half empty. Nobody tracks which dates went unsold, what discount was tried or which kind of event would fill them.
- cost: Fixed costs that do not go away: staff, power, maintenance and loan payments, all carried by the dates that did sell.
- fix: The owner’s view shows the year by date: sold, held and open. It compares revenue by month with last year, and lists open dates by how close they are, so offers or smaller events can be tried in time.
- screen: owner

## Day, before and after

Lede: The weddings are the same. What changes is that the plan, the money and the extras are written down once, where everyone can read them.

- 8:00: before: The manager checks the diary, the WhatsApp group and his memory to see what the day needs. | after: He opens the event file and the day checklist. Vendors due, staff and layout are all on the page.
- 10:30: before: A family asks about the 28th. The manager says it is free and later finds out a hold exists. | after: The calendar shows the 28th as held, with who holds it and when the hold ends.
- 3:00: before: The decorator and caterer both claim the loading bay. The manager sorts it out by phone. | after: The arrival times were fixed on the event file two days earlier, so the bay is clear in turn.
- 7:30: before: The family wants 80 more chairs. The manager agrees and nobody writes it down. | after: He logs the chairs with the time and quantity, and they are added to the bill.
- 11:45: before: The DJ plays an extra hour. The manager mentions it to the owner next day if he remembers. | after: Overtime shows against the agreed end time and is billed from the record.
- Next day: before: Plates are short, a panel is cracked and the deposit is argued over from memory. | after: The before and after checklist shows what changed, and the deposit is settled from it.

## How we work

Heading: Four weeks, started with the dates already in your diary.
Lede: We begin with the bookings you already have. We learn how your venue sells a date and runs an event, build Verity around that, move your calendar across, and stay on through your first busy season.

### Week 1: Discovery and mapping
- we: Spend a day at the venue with you and your manager. Walk the halls and lawn, follow one real booking from site visit to settlement, and note every hand that touches the money.
- you: Two to three hours from you and your manager. Your diary, the booking sheet and a few past event bills.
- get: A written map of how a date is sold and an event is run, your hall and event types, price rules and the problems we will fix first.

### Weeks 2 to 3: Configuration
- we: Set up your halls, lawns and slots, rate cards by season and day, payment schedules, cancellation terms, extras, order-sheet fields, checklists, staff roles and licence list.
- you: Check the setup with your manager. Tell us where a rate rule or checklist does not match how you really work.
- get: A working Verity with your halls, rates and checklists in your own words, ready for your calendar.

## Rules and paperwork

Note: Rules change and differ by town and state. Confirm current requirements with your accountant, the municipal office or the fire department.

- **Fire safety and local licences**: what: Banquet halls and lawns generally need a fire clearance and a municipal trade licence, and many places add loudspeaker, traffic and electrical clearances. Requirements differ by town. - **Food licence for catering**: what: A venue that prepares or serves food generally needs a food business licence, and the caterer may need its own. ## FAQ

Heading: Questions venue owners ask.

- **Will my sales team and manager really use it?** It works if each call, visit and payment is recorded, so we set it up so that entry takes seconds and sits where they already work. They get something back: no more double-booked dates and no more arguments about what was paid.
- **Does Verity let families book a date online?** No. Your team keeps taking bookings by call and visit. Verity records each booking, holds the date on the calendar and tracks everything that follows.
- **We have a hall, a lawn and a terrace. Can they be sold together or apart?** Yes. Each space is its own calendar and an event can use several. A date used by a large wedding shows as taken across the spaces it needs.
- **How do you handle cash advances taken by different people?** Each payment is recorded against the booking with the person who took it, the date and the mode. The balance is always visible, and cash taken without a booking attached is flagged for matching.
- **Can it work out the bill with extras and overtime?** It adds the extras and overtime logged on the day to the agreed amount, using the rates you set. You review and issue the final invoice through your usual accounts process.
- **Who can see what a family paid?** Only the people you allow. Access is set by role, so sales, the event supervisor and accounts each see what they need, and every change is recorded with who made it and when.
- **Do you handle several branches or venues?** Yes. Each venue is a location in one system. Calendars, dates sold, balances and licences can be compared side by side, and a family that books at two venues is one person.
- **Can Verity help in the slow months?** It shows which dates went unsold and how close they are, and keeps a list of past enquirers and families to offer them to. Your team makes the calls and decides on the offers.
- **How long before I see results?** The calendar with holds and the dues list only need your diary and receipts, so we aim to have them working first. Extras, stock losses and utilisation trends become reliable after a few events are recorded.
- **What does it cost?** It depends on the number of venues, spaces and staff. Message us and we will give you a clear number after a short conversation about how your venue runs.
